1. Introduction & Definition

Anorgasmia is defined as the persistent or recurrent difficulty, delay in, or absence of attaining orgasm after sufficient sexual stimulation and arousal. It is a distinct sexual dysfunction that affects both men and women, though it is more commonly reported in female patients. Despite its prevalence, anorgasmia remains significantly underdiagnosed because patients rarely volunteer this complaint and providers often fail to ask.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

Female Orgasmic Disorder (302.73)

  • Marked delay in, marked infrequency of, or absence of orgasm
  • Markedly reduced intensity of orgasmic sensations
  • Present for approximately 6 months
  • Causes clinically significant distress
  • Not better explained by another mental disorder, substance/medication, or medical condition

Specifiers: Lifelong vs. acquired; generalized vs. situational; mild/moderate/severe

Delayed Ejaculation (302.74)

  • Marked delay in, marked infrequency of, or absence of ejaculation
  • Present on approximately 75-100% of occasions of sexual activity
  • Duration of at least 6 months
  • Causes clinically significant distress
  • Not better explained by another condition, substance, or relationship distress

Specifiers: Lifelong vs. acquired; generalized vs. situational; mild/moderate/severe

Distinguishing from Other Sexual Dysfunctions

ConditionKey Distinction from Anorgasmia
HSDD (Low Libido)Absent or reduced desire/interest in sexual activity. Patients with anorgasmia may have normal desire but cannot reach climax.
Female Sexual Arousal DisorderInability to attain or maintain adequate lubrication/swelling. Arousal is impaired rather than orgasm specifically.
Erectile DysfunctionInability to achieve/maintain erection. Men with ED may still orgasm; men with anorgasmia can maintain erection but cannot climax.
Dyspareunia / VaginismusPain during intercourse. May coexist with anorgasmia but is a separate entity centered on genital pain rather than orgasmic failure.

Prevalence

  • Female: Approximately 10-15% of women have never experienced orgasm (primary anorgasmia). Up to 25% of women report persistent difficulty reaching orgasm. Situational anorgasmia (e.g., can orgasm alone but not with a partner) is significantly more common.
  • Male: Estimated at approximately 8% overall, though likely underreported due to stigma. Delayed ejaculation is the least common male sexual dysfunction but can be profoundly distressing.
  • Medication-induced: SSRI/SNRI-associated orgasmic dysfunction occurs in 30-70% of patients, making it the most common iatrogenic cause.
Clinical Pearl

Patients almost never volunteer information about orgasmic difficulties. Providers must proactively screen for sexual dysfunction using open-ended, normalizing language. Incorporating a brief sexual function screen into annual visits (e.g., “Many of my patients experience changes in sexual function. Is this something you’d like to discuss?”) dramatically increases detection rates.

Neuroanatomy of Orgasm

Peripheral Pathway

  • Pudendal nerve (S2-S4): Primary somatic nerve carrying sensory input from the genitalia to the spinal cord. Carries afferent signals from clitoris/penis and efferent motor signals to pelvic floor muscles.
  • Dorsal nerve of the clitoris/penis: Terminal sensory branch of the pudendal nerve; dense concentration of mechanoreceptors and free nerve endings.
  • Pelvic splanchnic nerves (S2-S4): Parasympathetic innervation for arousal (vasocongestion).
  • Hypogastric nerve (T10-L2): Sympathetic innervation critical for the emission phase in males.

Spinal Cord Reflex Arc

  • Sacral segments S2-S4: Integration center for the orgasmic reflex arc. Afferent sensory signals reach the sacral cord, triggering rhythmic motor output to the bulbocavernosus and ischiocavernosus muscles.
  • Spinal cord injuries above S2 may preserve reflex orgasm; injuries at S2-S4 typically abolish it.

Brain Centers

  • Medial preoptic area (MPOA): Hypothalamic integration center for sexual behavior; receives dopaminergic input.
  • Paraventricular nucleus (PVN): Releases oxytocin during orgasm; projects to sacral spinal cord to facilitate motor output.
  • Nucleus accumbens: Reward/pleasure center; dopaminergic activation during orgasm.
  • Prefrontal cortex: May exert inhibitory control; deactivation during orgasm correlates with subjective experience of “letting go.”

Neurotransmitter Balance in Orgasmic Function

Neurotransmitter Roles in Orgasm: Facilitation vs. Inhibition

Facilitate Orgasm

  • Dopamine — activates D2 receptors in MPOA; drives reward and motor output
  • Norepinephrine — sympathetic activation; triggers emission/expulsion
  • Oxytocin — released from PVN; enhances contractions and subjective pleasure
  • Nitric Oxide (NO) — vasodilation, genital engorgement, sensory amplification
BALANCE
disruption → anorgasmia

Inhibit Orgasm

  • Serotonin (5-HT) — 5-HT2A/2C receptor activation inhibits dopamine release and NO pathways
  • Prolactin — post-orgasm satiety signal; chronically elevated levels suppress sexual function
  • Endogenous opioids — high tonic levels suppress arousal/orgasm pathways
  • GABA — inhibitory tone in cortical circuits; excessive sedation impairs orgasm

Sex-Specific Orgasmic Physiology

Female Orgasm

  • Primarily clitoral: The clitoris (including the internal crura and bulbs) contains approximately 8,000+ nerve endings and is the primary orgasmic trigger
  • Only approximately 25% of women consistently orgasm from vaginal penetration alone
  • Vaginal (internal) orgasm: Likely involves indirect stimulation of internal clitoral structures and/or the anterior vaginal wall (“G-spot” area, Skene’s glands)
  • No refractory period in most women — capacity for multiple orgasms
  • Rhythmic contractions of pelvic floor muscles at 0.8-second intervals
  • Involves both sympathetic and somatic pathways

Male Orgasm

  • Emission phase: Sympathetic (T10-L2 via hypogastric nerve) — seminal fluid deposited into posterior urethra, bladder neck closes
  • Expulsion phase: Somatic (S2-S4 via pudendal nerve) — rhythmic contractions of bulbospongiosus and ischiocavernosus muscles
  • These two phases can be dissociated: orgasm without ejaculation (anejaculation) or ejaculation without orgasm (orgasmic anhedonia)
  • Refractory period present; increases with age
  • Penile glans is primary sensory trigger via dorsal nerve of penis
Key Takeaway

Understanding that female orgasm is predominantly clitoral (not vaginal) is essential for patient education. Many women and their partners have misconceptions about “normal” orgasm that lead to unnecessary distress. Similarly, understanding the two-phase nature of male orgasm helps differentiate anorgasmia from ejaculatory disorders.

Clinical Pearl

Always ask male patients specifically: “Can you reach climax?” separately from “Do you ejaculate?” These are physiologically distinct events. A man who ejaculates normally but reports no pleasure has orgasmic anhedonia, not anorgasmia. A man who has orgasmic sensation but no ejaculate may have retrograde ejaculation — check a post-orgasm urinalysis.

4. Etiology — Female

Psychological Factors

  • Sexual trauma / abuse history: One of the strongest predictors of anorgasmia. May cause dissociation during sexual activity, hypervigilance, inability to “let go.” Always screen sensitively.
  • Anxiety and depression: Both the conditions themselves and their treatments impair orgasm. Performance anxiety (“spectatoring”) is particularly disruptive — cognitive distraction prevents adequate arousal buildup.
  • Body image issues: Self-consciousness during sexual activity diverts attention from physical sensation.
  • Religious or cultural shame: Deeply internalized messages that sexual pleasure is wrong, dirty, or sinful can create powerful orgasmic inhibition even in the absence of other pathology.
  • Relationship conflict: Resentment, poor communication, lack of emotional intimacy, trust issues all contribute.
  • Performance pressure: Pressure from self or partner to orgasm creates a self-defeating cycle — anxiety about orgasm prevents orgasm.
  • Inadequate stimulation or technique: Lack of clitoral stimulation, insufficient foreplay, partner-focused (penetration-centric) sexual activity.

Medical Conditions

  • Diabetes mellitus: Peripheral neuropathy affecting pudendal nerve and pelvic autonomic nerves; also causes microvascular disease impairing genital blood flow.
  • Multiple sclerosis: Demyelination of sacral spinal cord and cerebral pathways involved in orgasm; up to 40% of women with MS report orgasmic dysfunction.
  • Spinal cord injury: Depending on level and completeness; sacral injuries (S2-S4) most directly impair orgasm.
  • Pelvic surgery: Hysterectomy (particularly total with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy), pelvic exenteration, colorectal surgery — may damage pelvic nerves.
  • Pelvic floor dysfunction:
    • Hypertonic: Overactive pelvic floor muscles cause pain and inhibit orgasmic contractions
    • Hypotonic: Weak muscles produce less satisfying or absent orgasmic contractions
  • Vulvovaginal atrophy: Decreased tissue integrity and reduced sensitivity from estrogen loss.

Hormonal Factors

  • Menopause / Estrogen decline: Decreased genital blood flow, reduced clitoral and vaginal sensitivity, vulvovaginal atrophy, thinning of mucosal tissue.
  • Low testosterone: Testosterone is critical for sexual desire and genital sensitivity in women. Declines with age, bilateral oophorectomy, adrenal insufficiency.
  • Hyperprolactinemia: Elevated prolactin (prolactinoma, medications) suppresses GnRH, leading to hypogonadism and direct inhibition of sexual function.
  • Hypothyroidism: Associated with decreased sexual desire and orgasmic dysfunction; mechanism involves altered neurotransmitter metabolism.
  • Oral contraceptives: Increase SHBG by 2-4x, binding free testosterone and reducing bioavailable androgen. Effect may persist after discontinuation (“post-pill SHBG”).

Medication-Induced

  • SSRIs/SNRIs: The number one cause of acquired anorgasmia. Serotonergic inhibition of dopamine and NO pathways. See Section 6 for deep dive.
  • Antipsychotics: Dopamine blockade (especially typical antipsychotics and risperidone) plus prolactin elevation.
  • Opioids: Suppress GnRH → hypogonadism; also direct CNS depression of orgasmic centers.
  • Anticonvulsants: Gabapentin, pregabalin, carbamazepine — reduce neural excitability; also induce hepatic enzyme metabolism of sex hormones.
  • Antihistamines: Anticholinergic effects reduce arousal and genital secretions.
  • Oral contraceptives: See hormonal factors above (SHBG elevation).

Anatomical Factors

  • Clitoral phimosis: Adhesions of the clitoral hood preventing adequate exposure and stimulation of the glans clitoris. May be congenital or secondary to lichen sclerosus.
  • Female genital mutilation (FGM): Partial or complete removal of clitoral tissue; direct destruction of primary orgasmic trigger. Affects over 200 million women worldwide.
  • Prior vulvar surgery: Vulvectomy, labiaplasty complications, episiotomy scarring — may damage nerve pathways.
Important

When evaluating acquired anorgasmia in women, always perform a thorough medication reconciliation. The temporal relationship between starting a new medication and onset of orgasmic difficulty is the strongest diagnostic clue for medication-induced anorgasmia. Ask specifically about SSRIs, SNRIs, antipsychotics, and any recent changes.

5. Etiology — Male

Psychological Factors

  • Performance anxiety: Fear of not satisfying a partner, history of sexual failure, excessive self-monitoring during sex.
  • Porn-induced desensitization: Increasingly recognized. Chronic exposure to high-intensity visual stimuli can raise the threshold for orgasmic response. Patients may require progressively more extreme or specific stimulation. Typically presents as situational (can orgasm to porn/masturbation but not with partner).
  • Relationship issues: Ambivalence toward partner, unconscious anger, sexual orientation conflict, intimacy avoidance.
  • Trauma history: Sexual abuse, assault, or deeply negative early sexual experiences.
  • “Idiosyncratic masturbatory style”: Masturbation with very specific pressure, speed, or technique that cannot be replicated during partnered sex.

Medical Conditions

  • Diabetes mellitus: The most common organic cause. Autonomic neuropathy affecting sympathetic emission pathways (T10-L2) and somatic ejaculatory pathways (S2-S4). Prevalence of ejaculatory dysfunction in diabetic men: 30-35%.
  • Multiple sclerosis: Spinal cord plaques disrupt the ejaculatory reflex arc.
  • Spinal cord injury: Level-dependent. Above T10: may lose psychogenic ejaculation but retain reflex ejaculation. Sacral injuries (S2-S4): lose reflex ejaculation.
  • Post-prostatectomy: Radical prostatectomy removes the prostate and seminal vesicles; anejaculation is expected. Nerve-sparing techniques may preserve orgasmic sensation but ejaculation is absent.
  • Penile neuropathy: Compression neuropathy from prolonged cycling (“cyclist’s syndrome”); dorsal nerve of penis compression against the saddle.
  • Pelvic surgery/radiation: Colorectal, bladder, or retroperitoneal surgery; pelvic radiation damages autonomic plexus.

Hormonal Factors

  • Low testosterone / Hypogonadism: Reduces desire, arousal, and ejaculatory threshold. Total testosterone <300 ng/dL warrants further evaluation.
  • Hyperprolactinemia: Directly inhibits GnRH → hypogonadism. Also has direct inhibitory effect on orgasmic pathways. Check prolactin in any male with unexplained acquired anorgasmia.
  • Hypothyroidism: Subclinical or overt; associated with delayed ejaculation.

Age-Related Changes

  • Decreased penile sensitivity: Progressive loss of nerve endings and receptor density with aging; requires more intense/prolonged stimulation.
  • Longer refractory periods: Increase from minutes (age 20s) to hours-days (age 60s+).
  • Reduced ejaculatory force and volume: Decline in pelvic floor muscle strength and seminal vesicle function.
  • Declining testosterone: Gradual decline (~1-2% per year after age 30) contributes to reduced orgasmic intensity.
Clinical Pearl

In younger men (<40) with acquired situational anorgasmia and normal hormones/labs, always ask about pornography use patterns and masturbation habits. Porn-induced delayed ejaculation is increasingly common and responds well to behavioral modification (gradual abstinence from pornography and retraining of masturbatory technique) without any pharmacologic intervention.

6. SSRI-Induced Anorgasmia — Deep Dive

SSRI-induced sexual dysfunction is one of the most common reasons patients discontinue their antidepressants. It deserves special attention because it is frequently encountered, often underreported, and has multiple evidence-based management strategies.

Mechanism

Serotonin’s inhibitory role: SSRIs increase synaptic serotonin, which activates 5-HT2A and 5-HT2C receptors throughout the CNS. This produces a cascade of inhibitory effects on orgasmic function:

  • Inhibition of dopamine release in the mesolimbic pathway (nucleus accumbens, VTA) — reduces pleasure/reward signaling
  • Inhibition of nitric oxide (NO) synthase — reduces genital vasocongestion and sensitivity
  • Direct inhibition of spinal ejaculatory reflex via 5-HT2C receptors in the sacral cord
  • Suppression of norepinephrine — reduces sympathetic activation needed for emission/expulsion
  • Peripheral sensory blunting — serotonin may directly reduce peripheral nerve sensitivity in genital tissue

Incidence and Reporting

  • When directly asked: 30-70% of SSRI users report orgasmic difficulty
  • Spontaneous reporting: Only 15-25% of affected patients volunteer this information
  • This 2-3x discrepancy means that providers who do not proactively ask are missing the majority of affected patients
  • Onset: Typically within 1-3 weeks of starting or dose increase; some patients notice immediately
  • Does not correlate well with antidepressant efficacy — patients can have full remission of depression but persistent orgasmic difficulty

Important

Post-SSRI Sexual Dysfunction (PSSD): An emerging and controversial entity in which sexual dysfunction (including anorgasmia) persists for months to years after SSRI discontinuation. Mechanism unclear but may involve epigenetic changes, receptor downregulation, or neuroplastic changes. Currently not well characterized but should be acknowledged to patients who report persistent symptoms after discontinuation. The EMA has added PSSD warnings to SSRI labeling.

Clinical Pearl

When starting a patient on an SSRI, proactively counsel about sexual side effects at the outset: “This medication can sometimes affect sexual function, including the ability to reach orgasm. If you notice any changes, please let me know — we have several strategies to address this.” This normalizes reporting, improves adherence, and gives patients permission to raise the issue later.

7. Clinical Evaluation

History Taking

The sexual history is the most important diagnostic tool for anorgasmia. Most patients will not volunteer this information, so the provider must create a safe, normalizing environment to facilitate disclosure.

Opening the Conversation

Normalizing lead-in: “I ask all my patients about sexual health because it’s an important part of overall well-being. Many people experience changes in sexual function at some point. Is this something you’ve noticed?”

Medication-specific: “Some medications, including [name of medication], can affect sexual function, including the ability to reach orgasm. Have you experienced any changes since starting this?”

Menopausal patients: “Many women going through the menopausal transition notice changes in their sexual function. This is very common and treatable. Would you like to discuss this?”

Key Assessment Questions

  • Lifelong vs. Acquired: “Have you ever been able to reach orgasm? When did the difficulty start?”
  • Situational vs. Generalized: “Can you reach orgasm in some situations but not others? (With a partner vs. alone? With one type of stimulation vs. another?)”
  • Timeline: “When did you first notice this? Was it gradual or sudden? Did anything change around that time (new medication, health issue, relationship change)?”
  • Medication relationship: “Did this start or worsen after beginning any new medications?”
  • Arousal vs. Orgasm: “Do you become physically aroused (lubrication/erection)? Is the problem specifically with reaching climax?”
  • Trauma screen: “Some people have had negative sexual experiences in the past that can affect their current sexual function. Is this something that may apply to you?” (Ask with sensitivity; do not press if patient declines.)
  • Distress: “How much does this bother you?” (Essential for diagnosis — DSM-5 requires clinically significant distress.)

Physical Examination

Female Examination

  • External inspection: Vulvar atrophy, pallor, dryness, lichen sclerosus, clitoral hood adhesions (phimosis), labial fusion, scarring
  • Clitoral assessment: Retract hood gently to assess glans visibility; note any adhesions, buried clitoris
  • Pelvic floor tone: Digital vaginal exam — assess resting tone (hypertonic vs. hypotonic), ability to contract/relax, tenderness, trigger points
  • Sensation testing: Light touch and pinprick to labia, perineum, perianal area (S2-S4 dermatomes)
  • Signs of atrophy: Thinning of vaginal mucosa, decreased rugae, petechiae, friability, loss of labial fullness
  • Vaginal pH: Elevated pH (>5.0) supports diagnosis of genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)

Male Examination

  • Genital exam: Penile anatomy, urethral meatus position, foreskin retractability (if uncircumcised), testicular size and consistency
  • Penile sensation: Light touch to glans and shaft; compare bilaterally. Biothesiometry if available (vibration threshold testing)
  • Bulbocavernosus reflex (BCR): Squeeze glans → observe anal sphincter contraction. Tests S2-S4 reflex arc integrity. Absent BCR suggests sacral neuropathy.
  • Cremasteric reflex: Stroke inner thigh → ipsilateral testicular elevation. Tests L1-L2 integrity.
  • DRE: If indicated — prostate size, consistency, tenderness. Relevant post-prostatectomy or if retrograde ejaculation suspected.
  • Signs of hypogonadism: Decreased body hair, gynecomastia, small testes, reduced muscle mass

Validated Questionnaires

Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI)

19-item self-report questionnaire assessing 6 domains over the prior 4 weeks:

  1. Desire (2 items)
  2. Arousal (4 items)
  3. Lubrication (4 items)
  4. Orgasm (3 items)
  5. Satisfaction (3 items)
  6. Pain (3 items)

Total score range: 2.0-36.0. Cutoff score ≤26.55 suggests sexual dysfunction. The orgasm domain specifically (items 11-13) asks about frequency, difficulty, and satisfaction with orgasm.

Arizona Sexual Experiences Scale (ASEX)

5-item brief screening tool assessing:

  1. Sex drive strength
  2. Ease of arousal
  3. Ability to achieve erection (M) / lubrication (F)
  4. Ease of reaching orgasm
  5. Satisfaction with orgasm

Each item scored 1-6 (1 = hyperfunction, 6 = hypofunction). Total score ≥19, any single item ≥5, or any three items ≥4 indicates sexual dysfunction. Quick, well-validated, useful for monitoring treatment response.

Key Takeaway

A structured evaluation approach — history (using normalizing language), focused physical exam, targeted lab workup, and validated questionnaire — allows systematic identification of the etiology and guides treatment selection. The most common mistake is failing to ask in the first place.

Non-Pharmacological Interventions (First-Line)

Directed Masturbation / Self-Exploration

The most evidence-based treatment for primary anorgasmia, with success rates up to 90% for achieving first orgasm. Developed by LoPiccolo and Lobitz (1972), refined over decades.

Stepwise approach:

  1. Visual and tactile self-examination of genitalia (using a mirror)
  2. Genital self-exploration focused on identifying pleasurable areas
  3. Concentrated manual stimulation of identified areas (clitoris, labia)
  4. If needed, addition of vibrator for enhanced stimulation
  5. Gradual integration of partner into the experience

This is often prescribed as “homework” in conjunction with sex therapy. Providing explicit permission and normalizing self-stimulation is therapeutic in itself for many patients.

Sensate Focus Therapy

Developed by Masters & Johnson. A structured couples exercise that removes performance pressure by prohibiting intercourse/orgasm initially.

  1. Phase 1: Non-genital touching only — focus on sensory experience, not arousal
  2. Phase 2: Include genital touching — still no orgasm as goal
  3. Phase 3: Gradual progression to orgasm-permitting activity

Reduces spectatoring and performance anxiety. Most effective when guided by a trained sex therapist.

Additional Non-Pharmacological Approaches

  • Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): Addresses negative cognitions about sex, body image, guilt, and performance anxiety. Evidence supports efficacy particularly when combined with directed masturbation.
  • Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy: For hypertonic pelvic floor — manual trigger point release, biofeedback, dilator therapy, stretching. For hypotonic pelvic floor — Kegel exercises, electrical stimulation, biofeedback to strengthen contractions.
  • Anatomical education: Many women lack basic knowledge of their own genital anatomy, particularly the clitoris. Educating patients that the clitoris (not the vagina) is the primary orgasmic organ and that most women require direct clitoral stimulation can be transformative.
  • Vibrator therapy: FDA-cleared Eros Clitoral Therapy Device (prescription device) applies suction to the clitoris, enhancing blood flow and engorgement. Commercial vibrators also effective and more accessible; normalize recommendation.
Clinical Pearl

For primary anorgasmia in women, directed masturbation should be considered the first-line treatment before any pharmacological intervention. It has the best evidence base (90% success rate), no side effects, empowers the patient with self-knowledge, and is effective regardless of the presence of a partner. Normalize the recommendation: “Self-exploration is the most effective way to learn what your body responds to. Many women find their first orgasm this way.”

9. Treatment — Male Anorgasmia

Non-Pharmacological Interventions

Sex Therapy / CBT

  • First-line for psychogenic anorgasmia (primary or acquired)
  • Address performance anxiety, spectatoring, relationship dynamics
  • Sensate focus exercises adapted for male patients
  • Cognitive restructuring of negative beliefs about sexual performance

Technique Modification

  • Vary stimulation patterns: Different manual techniques, oral stimulation, use of vibrator
  • Penile vibrostimulation: High-amplitude vibration (especially medical-grade devices at 2.5mm amplitude, 100Hz) can trigger ejaculatory reflex even in neurogenic cases. Evidence base primarily from spinal cord injury literature.
  • Reduce “death grip” pattern: If patient uses very tight grip during masturbation, gradual loosening and retraining with lighter touch

Address Porn-Induced Desensitization

When history suggests porn-induced delayed ejaculation:

  • Gradual reduction or abstinence from pornography
  • Masturbation retraining: reduce frequency, use lighter touch, eliminate “death grip”
  • Progressive exposure: masturbate with progressively less visual stimulation; shift to fantasy or partner-focused imagery
  • Timeline: Typically 4-12 weeks of behavioral modification before significant improvement
  • Discuss without judgment; frame as a neuroplasticity/habituation issue, not a moral one

Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy

  • Assess for hypertonic pelvic floor (paradoxical contraction during attempted ejaculation)
  • Biofeedback training for coordinated relaxation and contraction
  • Underutilized in male sexual dysfunction; growing evidence base

10. Special Considerations

Menopause Transition

  • Both estrogen and testosterone decline contribute to orgasmic difficulty during menopause
  • Estrogen loss: vulvovaginal atrophy, decreased genital blood flow, thinning of mucosal tissue, reduced clitoral sensitivity
  • Testosterone decline: gradual; ovaries continue producing some testosterone postmenopausally, but overall levels decrease approximately 50% between ages 20 and 45
  • Management: Local estrogen for atrophy (first-line); consider systemic HRT for moderate-severe symptoms; trial of testosterone therapy if desire/orgasm remain impaired after estrogen optimization
  • Do not dismiss sexual concerns as “just menopause” — these symptoms are treatable

Post-Hysterectomy

  • Controversial topic: Outcomes vary widely between patients
  • Some women report decreased orgasm intensity; others report no change or improvement (especially if hysterectomy resolved pain/bleeding)
  • Cervical orgasm is lost with total hysterectomy (supracervical spares the cervix)
  • Bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (BSO): Induces surgical menopause — abrupt loss of both estrogen and testosterone; significant impact on sexual function. Consider HRT including testosterone.
  • Nerve-sparing surgical techniques may preserve sexual function better, but data are limited
  • Counsel patients pre-operatively about potential effects on orgasm; discuss supracervical vs. total hysterectomy and ovarian preservation when appropriate

Pregnancy and Postpartum

  • Orgasmic difficulty is common during pregnancy (altered body image, physical discomfort, hormonal changes) and especially postpartum
  • Postpartum factors: Fatigue, hormonal shifts, perineal trauma/episiotomy healing, breastfeeding effects, body image changes, relationship stress, postpartum depression
  • Breastfeeding: Prolactin elevation suppresses estrogen → vaginal dryness, decreased genital sensitivity, reduced desire
  • Usually resolves gradually over 3-12 months postpartum
  • Reassure patients; recommend lubricant; evaluate for postpartum depression if persistent

Cancer Survivors

  • Chemotherapy: Premature ovarian failure, neuropathy, fatigue, body image distress
  • Pelvic radiation: Vaginal fibrosis, stenosis, dryness; penile vascular damage; autonomic nerve damage
  • Surgical effects: Radical hysterectomy, radical prostatectomy, abdominoperineal resection — direct nerve damage
  • Hormonal therapy: Aromatase inhibitors (severe estrogen depletion); androgen deprivation therapy (complete testosterone suppression in prostate cancer)
  • Sexual rehabilitation should be integrated into oncology survivorship care
  • Refer to sexual medicine specialists with oncology expertise when available

Pelvic Floor Dysfunction

  • Hypertonic pelvic floor: Overactive muscles create pain (dyspareunia), which inhibits orgasm; also impairs the coordinated rhythmic contractions necessary for orgasm. Common in patients with vulvodynia, chronic pelvic pain, endometriosis, history of trauma.
  • Hypotonic pelvic floor: Weak muscles produce diminished or absent orgasmic contractions. Common postpartum, post-menopausal, or after pelvic surgery.
  • Pelvic floor PT is significantly underutilized for sexual dysfunction. Many patients (and providers) are unaware that specialized pelvic floor therapists can assess and treat these conditions.
  • Treatment: Biofeedback, manual therapy, electrical stimulation, home exercise programs, dilator therapy (hypertonic), Kegel training (hypotonic)

Aging

  • Gradual changes in both sexes are normal and should be normalized while still evaluating for treatable causes
  • Women: Decreased blood flow, reduced sensitivity, longer time to arousal, less intense orgasms — but capacity for orgasm is generally preserved with adequate stimulation
  • Men: Decreased penile sensitivity, longer refractory periods, reduced ejaculatory force, need for more direct stimulation
  • Age alone is not a diagnosis — always evaluate for reversible contributing factors (medications, hormonal changes, medical conditions)
  • Reassure patients that sexual activity and orgasm can remain satisfying throughout the lifespan with appropriate adaptations

LGBTQ+ Considerations

  • Inclusive history-taking: Use gender-neutral language (“partner(s)” rather than “husband/wife”); ask about sexual practices without assumptions about anatomy or orientation
  • Do not assume anatomy = gender: Transgender patients may have natal or surgically constructed genitalia; assess based on current anatomy, hormone status, and surgical history
  • Transgender patients on hormone therapy:
    • Transfeminine patients on estrogen/anti-androgens: decreased erectile function, changes in orgasmic sensation, reduced ejaculate
    • Transmasculine patients on testosterone: clitoral growth, increased sensitivity, potential changes in orgasmic quality
  • Post-surgical considerations: Neovaginal and neophallic orgasmic capacity depends on nerve preservation during construction; counsel about realistic expectations
  • Different orgasmic patterns: Orgasm may occur through different types of stimulation (anal, nipple, mental) that differ from heteronormative assumptions; ask open-ended questions about what type of stimulation the patient has used
Key Takeaway

Special populations require nuanced evaluation and management. Avoid assumptions based on age, gender identity, sexual orientation, or medical/surgical history. Orgasmic dysfunction in these populations is common, often multifactorial, and frequently undertreated. A proactive, inclusive, and evidence-based approach improves outcomes.

12. Patient Communication Scripts

Script 1: Opening the Conversation (Routine Sexual Health Screening)

Provider Script

“I like to check in with all of my patients about sexual health, because it’s an important part of overall well-being that often doesn’t get addressed in medical visits. Many people experience changes in their sexual function at various points in life — things like changes in desire, arousal, or the ability to reach orgasm. These are very common and are often very treatable.

Is sexual function something that you have any concerns about? Even if it seems minor, I’d be happy to discuss it. There’s nothing that would surprise me or that I haven’t heard before.”

Key Takeaway

This script works because it: (1) normalizes the question by framing it as routine, (2) lists specific domains so patients know what “counts,” (3) validates that concerns are common and treatable, and (4) gives explicit permission to share. Asking “Any problems with sex?” is far less effective than this structured approach.

Script 2: Discussing Female Anorgasmia

Provider Script

“Thank you for sharing that with me. Difficulty reaching orgasm is actually one of the most common sexual concerns among women — it affects up to 1 in 4 women to some degree. It does not mean anything is ‘wrong’ with you.

There are many things that can contribute to this — it could be related to how your body responds to different types of stimulation, medications you’re taking, hormonal changes, stress, or other factors. The good news is that this is very treatable.

One thing that’s important to know is that the majority of women — about 75% — do not reach orgasm from intercourse alone. Most women need direct clitoral stimulation. This is normal anatomy, not a dysfunction. Many women find that exploring what feels good on their own first can be very helpful.

I’d like to understand a bit more about your experience so we can figure out the best approach. Can you tell me a bit more about when this started and what you’ve noticed?”

Script 3: Discussing SSRI-Induced Anorgasmia

Provider Script

“I’m glad you brought this up. What you’re describing — difficulty reaching orgasm since starting [medication name] — is actually a very well-known side effect of this type of medication. It happens to somewhere between 30-70% of people who take SSRIs. You are definitely not alone in this.

The medication works by increasing serotonin in the brain, which helps with mood and anxiety, but serotonin also has an inhibitory effect on the pathways involved in orgasm. It’s essentially doing exactly what it’s designed to do, but that effect extends to sexual function.

The important thing is that we have several options to address this without compromising your mental health treatment. We could:

• Adjust the dose of your current medication to the minimum effective level
• Add another medication that can counteract the sexual side effects
• Consider switching to a different antidepressant that has less impact on sexual function
• Explore timing strategies

I want you to know that your sexual well-being matters, and we can work together to find a balance. What sounds like a reasonable approach to you?”

Clinical Pearl

In all three scripts, notice the deliberate pattern: normalize first, educate second, offer solutions third, and empower the patient to participate in decision-making. Avoid clinical jargon when possible. The phrase “many people experience this” is far more powerful than statistics alone because it directly combats the patient’s feeling of being uniquely broken.
