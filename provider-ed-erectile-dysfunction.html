Erectile Dysfunction: Comprehensive Provider Guide

Clinical Reference for Nurse Practitioners and Primary Care Providers

1. Introduction and Epidemiology

Erectile dysfunction (ED) is defined as the persistent inability to achieve or maintain an erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual performance. While often perceived as a quality-of-life concern, ED has emerged as a clinically significant marker of systemic vascular disease and cardiovascular risk. The Massachusetts Male Aging Study (MMAS) remains the landmark epidemiological reference for ED prevalence.

Prevalence

  • Age 40: Approximately 40% of men report some degree of ED
  • Age 70: Prevalence increases to approximately 70%
  • Complete ED: Affects 5% of men at age 40, increasing to 15% by age 70
  • Global estimate: Over 150 million men affected worldwide; projected to exceed 320 million by 2025
  • Under-reported: Fewer than 25% of men with ED seek treatment
Clinical Pearl
ED is not an inevitable consequence of aging. While prevalence increases with age, the presence of ED at any age warrants clinical evaluation for modifiable risk factors and underlying cardiovascular disease.

ED as a Cardiovascular Sentinel Event

The penile arteries (1-2 mm diameter) are smaller than coronary arteries (3-4 mm) and other major vascular beds. Atherosclerotic plaque burden sufficient to compromise penile blood flow will typically precede clinically significant coronary obstruction. This concept, known as the "artery size hypothesis," explains why ED often serves as an early warning sign of systemic vascular disease.

  • ED precedes coronary artery disease events by an average of 3-5 years
  • Men with ED have a 1.6-fold increased risk of major cardiovascular events
  • ED in men aged 40-49 confers a cardiovascular risk equivalent to a 10-20 year age increase
  • ED severity correlates with the degree of coronary artery disease
Critical Clinical Implication
Every new diagnosis of ED should prompt cardiovascular risk assessment. The European Association of Urology (EAU) and the Princeton III Consensus recommend that men presenting with ED and no known cardiovascular disease should be evaluated as potential cardiac patients until proven otherwise.

Key Anatomical Structures

  • Corpora Cavernosa: Paired cylindrical erectile bodies composed of sinusoidal smooth muscle and endothelium-lined lacunar spaces. The primary structures responsible for rigidity.
  • Corpus Spongiosum: Surrounds the urethra; provides less rigidity but prevents urethral compression during erection.
  • Tunica Albuginea: Dense fibro-elastic envelope surrounding each corpus cavernosum. Critical for the veno-occlusive mechanism (trapping blood to maintain rigidity).
  • Cavernosal (Central) Arteries: Branches of the internal pudendal arteries; the primary arterial supply. Give off helicine arterioles that open directly into the sinusoidal spaces.
  • Dorsal Arteries: Supply the glans and penile skin.
  • Dorsal Vein: Primary drainage route; subtunical venules drain through the tunica into the deep dorsal vein.

Clinical Pearl
Venous leak occurs when the veno-occlusive mechanism fails—commonly due to smooth muscle degeneration, fibrosis, or tunica albuginea damage (as in Peyronie's disease). Patients with venous leak may achieve partial erection but cannot maintain rigidity. This represents a distinct pathophysiology from arterial insufficiency.

3. Pathophysiology

ED is best understood as a failure at one or more points along the erection pathway. Most patients have mixed etiology.

Vasculogenic (Most Common)

Arterial Insufficiency

  • Atherosclerosis of the internal pudendal or cavernosal arteries reduces arterial inflow
  • Endothelial dysfunction reduces NO bioavailability
  • Same pathology as coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease
  • Associated with: hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking, obesity

Venous Leak (Corporeal Veno-Occlusive Dysfunction)

  • Failure to trap blood due to impaired veno-occlusive mechanism
  • Causes: smooth muscle degeneration, Peyronie's disease, tunica albuginea abnormalities
  • Diagnosed via penile duplex ultrasound (elevated end-diastolic velocity)

Neurogenic

  • Peripheral neuropathy: Diabetes mellitus (most common cause), alcoholic neuropathy
  • Central lesions: Multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease, stroke, spinal cord injury
  • Surgical/trauma: Radical prostatectomy (cavernous nerve damage), pelvic fracture, cystectomy, colorectal surgery
  • Radiation: Pelvic radiation therapy damages neurovascular bundles

Hormonal (Endocrine)

  • Hypogonadism: Low testosterone reduces libido (desire) and may impair NO synthase expression. Total T <300 ng/dL warrants further evaluation
  • Hyperprolactinemia: Suppresses GnRH, reduces testosterone; can indicate pituitary adenoma
  • Thyroid disorders: Both hypo- and hyperthyroidism can contribute to ED
  • Diabetes mellitus: Combined hormonal, vascular, and neurogenic effects

Psychogenic

  • Performance anxiety: Sympathetic overdrive impairs parasympathetic-mediated erection
  • Depression: Both the condition and its treatment (SSRIs) contribute
  • Relationship conflict: Interpersonal stress, loss of attraction, communication breakdown
  • History of sexual trauma
  • Pornography-induced ED: Increasingly recognized in younger men; desensitization to real-world stimuli

Clinical Pearl
Always perform a thorough medication reconciliation. When possible, consider switching to medications with fewer sexual side effects (e.g., bupropion instead of SSRIs, nebivolol instead of atenolol, ACE inhibitors or ARBs instead of thiazides). Never abruptly discontinue medications without clinical justification.

4. Classification: Organic vs. Psychogenic vs. Mixed

Organic ED

  • Onset: Gradual
  • Nocturnal erections: Absent or reduced
  • Partner-specific: No (global)
  • Masturbation: Also impaired
  • Libido: Often preserved (unless hormonal)
  • Risk factors: Vascular, metabolic, medication, surgery
  • Course: Progressive worsening

Psychogenic ED

  • Onset: Sudden (often tied to event)
  • Nocturnal erections: Present and normal
  • Partner-specific: Often yes (situational)
  • Masturbation: Erections intact
  • Libido: Variable
  • Risk factors: Anxiety, depression, relationship stress, new partner
  • Course: Fluctuating

Mixed ED

  • Onset: Variable
  • Nocturnal erections: Reduced but present
  • Partner-specific: Variable
  • Masturbation: Partially impaired
  • Libido: Variable
  • Risk factors: Combination of organic + psychological
  • Course: Most common clinical presentation
Key Takeaway
Most patients presenting in primary care have mixed etiology. Even when organic factors predominate, the psychological impact of ED creates a reinforcing cycle of performance anxiety. Addressing both components improves outcomes.

5. Risk Factors

Cardiovascular / Metabolic

  • Diabetes mellitus: 35-75% prevalence of ED; combined vascular + neurogenic injury. Onset 10-15 years earlier than age-matched controls
  • Hypertension: 2x increased risk; both the disease and many antihypertensives contribute
  • Hyperlipidemia: Directly impairs endothelial function
  • Obesity: Associated with low testosterone, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammation (BMI >30 doubles risk)
  • Metabolic syndrome: Each component independently increases ED risk
  • Coronary artery disease: Shared pathophysiology

Lifestyle and Other

  • Smoking: 1.5x risk; dose-dependent; partially reversible with cessation
  • Excessive alcohol: Acute and chronic neurotoxicity; HPG axis suppression
  • Sedentary lifestyle: Independent risk factor; exercise is protective (150+ min/week moderate activity reduces ED risk by 30-40%)
  • BPH/LUTS: Strong epidemiological link independent of age; shared smooth muscle and autonomic dysfunction
  • Peyronie's disease: Plaque formation causes ED in 20-45% of affected men
  • Pelvic surgery / radiation: Prostatectomy, cystectomy, colorectal surgery, radiation
  • Sleep apnea: Underrecognized contributor; improves with CPAP

6. Clinical Evaluation

History: The SHIM / IIEF-5 Questionnaire

First-Line Labs (All Patients)

  • Total testosterone (fasting, AM draw before 10:00)
  • Free/bioavailable testosterone (if total T borderline 250-400)
  • CBC
  • CMP (renal/hepatic function, glucose)
  • Lipid panel
  • HbA1c
  • TSH

Second-Line Labs (As Indicated)

  • Prolactin (if low T, low libido, or visual symptoms)
  • LH / FSH (distinguish primary vs. secondary hypogonadism)
  • PSA (before initiating TRT; per screening guidelines)
  • DHEA-S (adrenal contribution)
  • Estradiol (if on TRT or obese)
  • Penile duplex ultrasound (arterial insufficiency vs. venous leak)
  • Nocturnal penile tumescence (NPT) (rare; differentiates organic vs. psychogenic)

Cardiovascular Risk Stratification: Princeton III Consensus

Before initiating ED treatment, assess cardiovascular fitness for sexual activity. Sexual intercourse represents approximately 3-5 METs of exertion (similar to brisk walking or climbing 2 flights of stairs).

Low Risk

Can initiate/resume sexual activity and ED treatment.

  • Controlled hypertension
  • Mild, stable angina
  • Successful coronary revascularization
  • Mild valvular disease
  • LVD/CHF (NYHA Class I)
  • Post-MI >6-8 weeks (asymptomatic, able to perform ≥3 METs)
Intermediate Risk

Requires further cardiac evaluation (e.g., exercise stress test) before ED treatment.

  • ≥3 cardiac risk factors (excluding gender)
  • Moderate, stable angina
  • Recent MI (2-6 weeks)
  • LVD/CHF (NYHA Class II)
  • Non-cardiac sequelae of atherosclerosis (PAD, stroke hx)
High Risk

Defer ED treatment. Stabilize cardiac condition first. Cardiology referral required.

  • Unstable or refractory angina
  • Uncontrolled hypertension
  • Recent MI (<2 weeks)
  • LVD/CHF (NYHA Class III-IV)
  • High-risk arrhythmias
  • Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy
  • Moderate-severe valvular disease

1 Lifestyle Modification + Risk Factor Management

Inflatable penile prosthesis (IPP) is the gold standard surgical option. 3-piece inflatable: highest satisfaction rates (92-98% patient/partner satisfaction). Clinical Pearl
Combination therapy is underutilized and effective. Examples: PDE5i + testosterone replacement in hypogonadal men; PDE5i + vacuum device; low-dose daily tadalafil + on-demand sildenafil. Always optimize Step 1 throughout—lifestyle modification augments all other therapies.

Absolute Contraindications
  • Concurrent nitrate use (ANY form): Nitroglycerin (sublingual, patch, spray, IV), isosorbide mononitrate/dinitrate, amyl nitrite ("poppers"). Can cause severe, life-threatening hypotension. Must wait 24 hours after sildenafil/vardenafil/avanafil, and 48 hours after tadalafil before administering nitrates.

Relative Contraindications / Cautions
  • Alpha-blockers: Can potentiate hypotension. If needed, use tamsulosin (most uroselective) and start PDE5i at lowest dose with at least 4 hours between doses. Tadalafil 5 mg daily can be combined with tamsulosin (FDA-studied).

  • Recent stroke or MI (<6 months for most PDE5i)
  • Significant hepatic impairment: Dose reduction required
  • Hereditary retinopathies (e.g., retinitis pigmentosa)
  • History of priapism or conditions predisposing to priapism (sickle cell disease, leukemia, multiple myeloma)

Counseling Points for PDE5i Prescribing

  1. Sexual stimulation is required. PDE5 inhibitors do not cause spontaneous erection; they facilitate the natural mechanism.
  2. Adequate trial = 4-6 attempts at optimal dose with proper timing before declaring failure.
  3. Timing matters: Take sildenafil/vardenafil on an empty stomach for best effect. Tadalafil and avanafil are more forgiving with food.
  4. Dose titration: Start at standard dose, increase to maximum if tolerated and needed before switching agents.
  5. Do not combine PDE5 inhibitors with each other.
  6. Seek emergency care for erection lasting >4 hours (priapism).
  7. Report sudden vision or hearing loss (rare but reportable: NAION, sudden sensorineural hearing loss).
  8. Generic sildenafil and tadalafil are now available and significantly reduce cost.
Clinical Pearl
Tadalafil 5 mg daily is the only PDE5 inhibitor FDA-approved for both ED and BPH/LUTS. For men with both conditions, it is an efficient single-agent approach. The daily dosing also eliminates the need to time medication around sexual activity, which many patients prefer.

9. Second-Line Therapies

Intracavernosal Injection (ICI) Therapy

The most effective non-surgical treatment for ED. Patient self-injects directly into the lateral corpus cavernosum using a fine (27-30g) needle. Erection occurs in 5-15 minutes regardless of sexual stimulation.

Formulations

AgentComponentsTypical Starting DoseEfficacy
Alprostadil (Caverject/Edex) PGE1 alone 5-20 mcg 70-80%
Bimix Papaverine + Phentolamine 0.25-0.5 mL 60-70%
Trimix Papaverine + Phentolamine + PGE1 0.1-0.3 mL (compound pharmacy) 90-95%

Injection Technique (Key Points for Patient Education)

  • Inject into lateral aspect of the proximal or mid-shaft (avoid dorsal and ventral)
  • Alternate sides with each injection
  • Compress injection site for 3-5 minutes to prevent hematoma
  • Maximum frequency: 3x per week, with at least 24 hours between injections
  • First injection should be done in-office with monitoring (dose titration)
Priapism Protocol

Priapism = erection lasting >4 hours. This is a urological emergency. Risk of irreversible ischemic damage to corporal tissue if untreated beyond 4-6 hours.

Patient instructions: If erection persists >3 hours, apply ice packs, attempt physical exercise (climbing stairs), and take oral pseudoephedrine 60 mg. If >4 hours, proceed to emergency department.

ED treatment: Aspiration of blood from corpus cavernosum followed by injection of phenylephrine (100-500 mcg diluted, repeated every 3-5 minutes, max 1 mg in 1 hour). Monitor blood pressure and heart rate.

Intraurethral Alprostadil (MUSE)

  • Mechanism: Alprostadil pellet inserted into the urethra; absorbed through corpus spongiosum to corpora cavernosa
  • Dosing: 125, 250, 500, 1000 mcg pellets
  • Efficacy: 30-65% (less effective than ICI)
  • Advantages: Non-injection option; no needle phobia issue
  • Disadvantages: Urethral pain/burning (30%), penile pain, dizziness, less reliable than ICI
  • Technique: Urinate first (lubricates urethra), insert applicator 3 cm, release pellet, roll penis between hands for 10 seconds to distribute

Vacuum Erection Devices (VED)

  • Mechanism: Creates negative pressure around penis, drawing blood into corpora; constriction ring placed at base to trap blood
  • Efficacy: 60-80% satisfaction (varies with proper use)
  • Advantages: Non-pharmacologic, non-invasive, no systemic side effects, can be used with PDE5i
  • Disadvantages: Erection feels "cold" and pivots at base; constriction ring limits ejaculation in some men; requires planning; may cause petechiae or bruising
  • Constriction ring limit: 30 minutes maximum to prevent ischemia
  • Especially useful in: Post-prostatectomy penile rehabilitation, patients on anticoagulation (avoids injection risk), combination with PDE5i

Low-Intensity Shockwave Therapy (Li-ESWT)

  • Mechanism: Low-intensity extracorporeal shockwaves promote neovascularization and improve endothelial function in penile tissue
  • Protocol: Typically 6-12 sessions over 6-9 weeks
  • Evidence: Multiple RCTs show improvement in IIEF scores for mild-moderate vasculogenic ED; may restore spontaneous erections in some PDE5i responders; not FDA-approved for ED in the US (considered experimental/off-label)
  • Best candidates: Mild-moderate vasculogenic ED with some residual function
  • Limitations: Not covered by insurance; variable treatment protocols across studies; long-term durability data still emerging

10. Testosterone and ED

When Low T is Contributing vs. Coincidental

The relationship between testosterone and erectile function is nuanced. Testosterone is necessary but not sufficient for normal erections. Its primary role is in maintaining libido, and it also supports NO synthase expression in penile tissue.

Low T Likely Contributing to ED When:

  • Reduced libido is the primary complaint
  • Total T consistently <300 ng/dL
  • Morning/nocturnal erections absent or diminished
  • Other hypogonadal symptoms present (fatigue, reduced muscle mass, mood changes, decreased body hair)
  • ED occurred concurrently with testosterone decline
  • PDE5i response has been suboptimal

Low T Likely Coincidental When:

  • Libido is preserved
  • Total T borderline (300-400 ng/dL) without symptoms
  • ED is clearly situational/psychogenic
  • Strong vascular risk factors explain ED
  • Nocturnal erections present
  • T levels only mildly low in the setting of obesity (which independently lowers T)
Key Takeaway
Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) alone rarely restores erectile function in men with moderate-severe ED. However, restoring eugonadal testosterone levels significantly improves PDE5 inhibitor response in hypogonadal men who were previously poor responders. The combination of TRT + PDE5i is evidence-based for hypogonadal men with ED.

Monitoring on TRT

If initiating testosterone replacement, monitor per standard guidelines:

  • Baseline: Total/free T, CBC, PSA, CMP, lipid panel, DXA if indicated
  • 3-6 months: Total T (trough level), CBC (hematocrit), PSA, symptom assessment
  • Ongoing: Hematocrit every 6-12 months (target <54%); PSA annually; lipid panel annually
  • Counsel regarding: Fertility suppression (exogenous T suppresses spermatogenesis), erythrocytosis risk, sleep apnea exacerbation

For comprehensive testosterone management, refer to the dedicated Testosterone and Hormonal Health Provider Guide.

11. Psychogenic Erectile Dysfunction

Assessment Approach

Psychogenic ED should be considered when the clinical picture does not fit a purely organic etiology. Key features include sudden onset, situational nature, preserved nocturnal/morning erections, and identifiable psychosocial stressors.

Clinical Clues Suggesting Psychogenic Component

  • Age <40 without significant vascular risk factors
  • Sudden onset (especially linked to life event, new relationship, or conflict)
  • Erections normal during masturbation or with different partners
  • Normal morning erections
  • Able to initiate erection but loses it upon penetration attempt (performance anxiety pattern)
  • History of depression, anxiety, PTSD, or sexual trauma
  • Excessive pornography use with difficulty achieving arousal with real partner
  • Relationship discord or recent partner change

Evidence for Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

  • CBT is the best-studied psychological intervention for psychogenic ED
  • Targets maladaptive thought patterns (catastrophizing, spectatoring) and avoidance behaviors
  • Meta-analyses show significant improvement in erectile function and sexual satisfaction
  • Combination of CBT + PDE5i is superior to either alone for psychogenic and mixed ED
  • Sensate focus techniques (Masters & Johnson) remain a cornerstone of sex therapy

When to Refer for Sex Therapy / Counseling

  • Psychogenic ED that does not respond to PDE5i trial
  • Significant relationship distress contributing to or resulting from ED
  • History of sexual trauma
  • Performance anxiety as primary driver
  • Pornography-related ED in young men
  • Patient preference for non-pharmacologic approach
  • Depression or anxiety requiring management beyond primary care scope

Referral options: Certified sex therapist (AASECT), psychologist specializing in sexual dysfunction, couples therapist. Normalize the referral as part of comprehensive treatment.

12. Special Populations

Post-Prostatectomy ED

ED occurs in 25-75% of men following radical prostatectomy, even with bilateral nerve-sparing technique. Cavernous nerve injury (neuropraxia) is the primary mechanism. Recovery can take 18-24 months.

Penile Rehabilitation Protocols

  • Goal: Maintain oxygenation of corporal smooth muscle, prevent fibrosis, and facilitate nerve recovery
  • First-line: Daily low-dose PDE5i (tadalafil 5 mg daily or sildenafil 25 mg nightly) starting within weeks of catheter removal
  • Adjunctive: Vacuum erection device (10 minutes daily without constriction ring for tissue oxygenation)
  • If insufficient: Intracavernosal injections (low-dose alprostadil or Trimix) 2-3x per week
  • Timeline: Begin as early as possible post-operatively; continue for at least 18-24 months
  • Counseling: Set realistic expectations. Full recovery may not occur. Discuss prosthesis option if no improvement by 18-24 months.

Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes is the single most common organic cause of ED, affecting 35-75% of diabetic men. The mechanism is multifactorial: microangiopathy, peripheral neuropathy, endothelial dysfunction, and associated hypogonadism.

  • ED onset 10-15 years earlier than age-matched non-diabetic men
  • Severity correlates with HbA1c levels and duration of diabetes
  • PDE5 inhibitor response is lower in diabetic patients (56-72%) compared to general population (70-80%), but still first-line
  • Glycemic optimization improves ED outcomes and PDE5i response
  • Always check testosterone (diabetes independently lowers T)
  • Higher rates of progression to second-line therapies

Cardiovascular Patients

Use the Princeton III Consensus guidelines (Section 6) to stratify risk before initiating ED treatment. Key considerations:

  • Low-risk patients: Treat in primary care per standard algorithm
  • Intermediate-risk patients: Exercise stress test first; if can achieve ≥3-5 METs without symptoms, reclassify as low risk
  • High-risk patients: Stabilize cardiac status before ED treatment; cardiology co-management
  • Nitrate users: PDE5i absolutely contraindicated. Options include ICI therapy, VED, or penile prosthesis. If nitrates can be safely discontinued (cardiology collaboration), PDE5i may become an option
  • CABG / stent patients: Generally low risk once stable and asymptomatic post-procedure

Young Men (<40 years)

ED prevalence in men under 40 is increasing, with estimates of 8-30% depending on the population studied. The etiology differs from older men.

  • Most common causes: Psychogenic (performance anxiety, pornography-related ED), lifestyle factors (obesity, sedentary behavior, substance use), undiagnosed anxiety/depression
  • Organic causes to rule out: Undiagnosed diabetes or metabolic syndrome, hormonal abnormalities (varicocele, Klinefelter's), Peyronie's disease, pelvic injury (cycling)
  • Approach: Thorough psychosocial assessment; basic labs including testosterone and metabolic panel; address lifestyle factors aggressively; consider sex therapy referral early
  • PDE5i may be used short-term as a "bridge" to restore confidence while addressing underlying causes, but avoid long-term dependency without addressing root cause
  • If prescribing PDE5i to young men: Discuss that this is not a cure; emphasize concurrent lifestyle changes and psychological work

Failed PDE5i therapy After adequate trial of at least 2 different PDE5 inhibitors at maximum dose with proper technique (minimum 4-6 attempts each). Urology can offer ICI, MUSE, penile duplex ultrasound, and surgical options.
Peyronie's disease Penile curvature, plaque, pain with erection. Specialized treatment options include intralesional injection (collagenase/verapamil), traction therapy, and surgical correction.
Penile prosthesis candidates Patients interested in surgical solution after conservative measures fail. Requires experienced implanting urologist.
Complex hormonal issues Pituitary pathology, Klinefelter's syndrome, complex hypogonadism not responding to standard TRT. May co-refer with endocrinology.
Priapism Any episode of prolonged erection (>4 hours), stuttering priapism, or history of ischemic priapism. Requires urologic evaluation and management plan.
Young men with organic ED Men <40 with evidence of arterial insufficiency or venous leak on workup warrant specialized vascular/urologic evaluation.
Post-prostatectomy ED not responding to rehab After 18-24 months of penile rehabilitation without adequate recovery.
Penile trauma or anatomical abnormality Penile fracture, urethral injury, congenital curvature.

14. Patient Communication Scripts

Discussing ED requires a compassionate, normalizing approach. Many patients feel embarrassed, and the provider's initial response sets the tone for open dialogue. These scripts are guides—adapt to your conversational style.

Script 1: Opening the Conversation (Provider-Initiated Screening)
"As part of my routine health assessment, I ask all my male patients about sexual health. Many men experience changes in their sexual function at some point, and it can often be an important indicator of overall health. Have you noticed any changes in your ability to get or maintain erections? This is a completely confidential conversation, and there are effective treatments available."
Script 2: Normalizing After Patient Disclosure
"Thank you for bringing this up—I know it's not always easy to talk about. I want you to know that erectile difficulty is very common. It affects nearly half of all men by their 40s, and it's not something you have to just live with. The good news is that we have a clear approach to figuring out what's going on and several effective treatment options. What I also want you to know is that sometimes ED can be an early sign of other health issues like heart disease or diabetes, so addressing this is actually an important part of taking care of your overall health."
Script 3: Discussing Treatment Options
"Based on what we've discussed and your lab results, here's what I'd recommend. First, let's talk about some lifestyle changes that can make a real difference—things like regular exercise, dietary changes, and [specific modifications]. At the same time, I'd like to start you on a medication called [drug name]. This works by enhancing your body's natural response to sexual stimulation. It's not an aphrodisiac—you still need to be aroused—but it helps your body respond the way it should. I want you to give it a fair trial: take it [timing instructions], and try it at least four to six times before we assess how well it's working. If this approach doesn't give you the results you want, there are additional options we can explore. How does that sound? Do you have any questions?"
Clinical Pearl
Include the partner in the conversation when appropriate and with the patient's permission. Partner involvement improves treatment adherence, satisfaction, and outcomes. Frame ED as a shared concern rather than an individual failing.
