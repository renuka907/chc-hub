1. Professional Communication Basics

Every patient interaction shapes their experience with our clinic. Whether in person or on the phone, how you communicate matters as much as what you communicate. Patients may forget the specifics of their visit, but they will always remember how you made them feel.

Tone of Voice

Your tone sets the emotional temperature of the entire interaction. Speak calmly, warmly, and at a moderate pace. Avoid sounding rushed, bored, or irritated, even when you are busy or dealing with a challenging situation. A warm, steady voice immediately puts patients at ease and builds trust.

  • Calm and steady — patients mirror the energy you project
  • Warm but professional — friendly without being overly casual
  • Moderate pace — not so fast that patients feel rushed, not so slow that they feel patronized
  • Clear enunciation — especially important on the phone where visual cues are absent

Empathy and Active Listening

Empathy means acknowledging the patient's feelings without judgment. Active listening means giving your full attention, letting the patient finish speaking, and reflecting back what you heard to confirm understanding.

  • Let them finish speaking — never interrupt, even if you already know the answer
  • Reflect back — "So what I'm hearing is..." or "It sounds like you're experiencing..."
  • Validate feelings — "That sounds really frustrating" or "I can understand why that concerns you"
  • Avoid minimizing — never say "it's not a big deal" or "don't worry about it"

Clinical Pearl: The phrase "I understand" is one of the most powerful tools in patient communication. It does not mean you agree with everything the patient says. It means you acknowledge their experience and are ready to help.

Body Language (In-Person)

When interacting face-to-face, nonverbal cues account for a significant portion of communication. Be mindful of the signals you send.

  • Make eye contact — shows you are engaged and present
  • Face the patient — turn your body toward them, not toward the computer screen
  • Uncrossed arms — open posture conveys approachability
  • Nod occasionally — confirms you are listening
  • Respect personal space — maintain an appropriate distance

Professional Boundaries

You are a compassionate professional, not a friend. Maintaining boundaries protects both you and the patient.

  • Never argue with a patient — if a disagreement escalates, involve your supervisor
  • Stay within your scope — do not diagnose, interpret results, or give medical advice
  • Avoid sharing personal opinions about treatments, medications, or other providers
  • Keep personal life separate — patients may ask personal questions; redirect politely
  • Document, do not debate — if a patient disagrees with a policy, document the concern and escalate

Key Takeaway: Professional communication is not about being perfect. It is about being present, patient, and respectful in every interaction. When in doubt, slow down, listen, and ask for help.

2. Phone Triage — General Principles

Phone triage is one of the most critical responsibilities of a medical assistant. You are the first point of contact for patients who may be anxious, in pain, or in an emergency. How you handle that call can directly impact patient outcomes.

The Five Rules of Phone Triage

Rule 1: Answer by the Third Ring

Every unanswered ring increases patient anxiety. If you cannot answer immediately, ensure the phone system routes to voicemail with a professional greeting. Return all voicemails within 2 hours during business hours.

Rule 2: Identify Yourself and the Clinic

Always open with a greeting that includes the clinic name, your name, and your role. This immediately tells the patient they have reached the right place and who they are speaking with.

Standard Greeting Script

"Thank you for calling [Clinic Name], this is [Your Name], medical assistant. How can I help you today?"

Rule 3: Document Every Call

Every incoming patient call must be documented in the medical record or call log, regardless of how minor it seems. If it is not documented, it did not happen. This protects the patient, the provider, and you.

Rule 4: Never Diagnose or Give Medical Advice

Your role is to gather information, triage urgency, and route appropriately. You are never to tell a patient what their symptoms mean, what condition they might have, or what medication to take. Phrases like "it sounds like you might have..." are outside your scope.

Rule 5: Know When to Escalate

Some calls require immediate involvement of a provider or nurse. Trust your instincts. If something feels urgent, it probably is. It is always better to escalate unnecessarily than to miss something critical.

Warning: If a patient describes symptoms that could indicate a medical emergency — chest pain, difficulty breathing, severe bleeding, loss of consciousness, signs of stroke — instruct them to call 911 immediately. Do not place them on hold. Do not transfer the call. Direct them to emergency services first.

Escalation Criteria

Urgency Level Examples Action
Emergency Chest pain, difficulty breathing, stroke symptoms, severe allergic reaction, uncontrolled bleeding Direct to 911 immediately
Urgent Severe vomiting/dehydration, high fever, spreading infection signs, inability to eat or drink, severe pain Same-day appointment or after-hours ER referral
Semi-Urgent Breakthrough bleeding, moderate side effects, injection site concerns, new or worsening symptoms Schedule within 1-3 days; message provider
Routine Refill requests, scheduling, lab result inquiries, general questions, new patient inquiries Schedule per availability; route to appropriate team

3. Phone Triage Scripts

The following scripts cover the most common patient call scenarios at this clinic. Each scenario includes the questions you should ask, the red flags to watch for, and the appropriate action to take. Use these as a guide, not a rigid script — adapt your language to be natural while ensuring you gather all the necessary information.

Scenario A: "I'm Having Breakthrough Bleeding on My Pellets"

Breakthrough bleeding is one of the most common calls in a hormone therapy clinic. Your job is to gather enough information for the provider to determine the appropriate next step.

Questions to Ask

  • When did the bleeding start? — note exact date or approximate timeframe
  • How heavy is the bleeding? — is it spotting (a few drops, only when wiping) or soaking pads/tampons? How many pads per day?
  • Are you experiencing any pain or cramping? — note location, severity (1-10), and whether it is constant or intermittent
  • When was your last pellet insertion? — note the date
  • Are you taking your progesterone as prescribed? — confirm dose, frequency, and whether they have missed any doses
  • Have you had breakthrough bleeding before with pellets? — previous history helps with clinical context

Triage Action

Urgent / Same Day

Heavy bleeding (soaking more than 1 pad per hour), significant pain, dizziness, or lightheadedness. Message provider immediately and schedule same-day appointment.

Routine / Within 1 Week

Spotting or light bleeding, no significant pain, patient is hemodynamically stable. Document call, message provider, and schedule follow-up within one week.

Clinical Pearl: Always document the call and message the provider, even if the bleeding sounds mild. The provider may want to adjust progesterone dosing, check labs, or move up the next pellet insertion. Never assume spotting is "no big deal" — let the provider make that determination.

Scenario B: "I'm Having Side Effects from My GLP-1 Shot"

GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide and tirzepatide) commonly cause gastrointestinal side effects, especially during dose titration. Your role is to determine whether symptoms are expected and manageable or whether they require immediate evaluation.

Questions to Ask

  • Which medication are you taking? — semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) or tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound)
  • What dose are you on, and when did you last inject? — note if they recently titrated up
  • What side effects are you experiencing? — nausea, vomiting, constipation, diarrhea, bloating, abdominal pain
  • How severe are the symptoms on a scale of 1-10?
  • Are you able to keep food and water down? — this is a critical question for determining dehydration risk
  • Do you have any abdominal pain? — if yes, where exactly? Is it severe, constant, or radiating to the back?
  • How long have the symptoms been going on?

Triage Action

Urgent / Same Day or ER

Severe vomiting (cannot keep anything down for 24+ hours), inability to eat or drink, severe or worsening abdominal pain (especially radiating to back — possible pancreatitis), signs of dehydration (dark urine, dizziness, rapid heart rate). Schedule same-day; if after hours, direct to ER.

Routine / Reassure + Follow-Up

Mild to moderate nausea (especially in first few weeks or after dose increase), mild constipation, mild bloating. Reassure patient these are common and often temporary. Document and schedule follow-up. Provider may adjust dose or timing.

Warning: Severe, constant abdominal pain radiating to the back could indicate pancreatitis, a known rare but serious side effect of GLP-1 medications. This requires same-day evaluation or ER referral. Do not reassure the patient that this is normal.

Scenario C: "My Testosterone Injection Site Is Red and Swollen"

Local injection site reactions are common with testosterone injections. However, it is essential to distinguish a normal inflammatory response from signs of infection.

Questions to Ask

  • When did you give yourself the injection? — note how many hours or days ago
  • Where did you inject? — gluteal, thigh, deltoid
  • How large is the red/swollen area? — ask them to measure in inches or compare to a common object
  • Is the area warm or hot to the touch?
  • Do you have a fever? — ask for an actual temperature if they have a thermometer
  • Is there any drainage from the site? — clear, cloudy, or blood-tinged
  • Do you see any red streaking extending away from the injection site?

Triage Action

Normal / Monitor

Small area of redness (less than 2 inches), occurred within 24-48 hours of injection, no fever, no drainage, not getting worse. Reassure patient this is a common local reaction. Apply warm compress. Monitor for worsening. Call back if it spreads, they develop fever, or symptoms worsen.

Urgent / Same Day

Spreading redness (growing beyond 2 inches or getting larger), fever (100.4 F or higher), drainage (especially cloudy or foul-smelling), increasing pain or swelling. Schedule same-day appointment. Message provider immediately.

Warning: Red streaking extending away from the injection site is a sign of possible lymphangitis (spreading infection) and requires urgent or emergency evaluation. Direct the patient to urgent care or the ER if the clinic cannot see them immediately.

Scenario D: "I Want to Start Hormone Therapy"

New patient inquiries are an opportunity to make a great first impression. Be warm, informative, and organized.

Recommended Script

New Patient Script

"We'd love to help you! For new patients, we start with a comprehensive consultation where [provider] will review your symptoms, medical history, and order lab work. The first visit is about [60] minutes. You'll want to bring your ID, insurance card, a list of current medications, and any recent lab work you have. Shall I schedule you?"

Additional Information to Cover

  • Confirm what type of hormone therapy they are interested in (pellets, injections, creams, etc.) — note this for the scheduling team
  • Let them know if there is a new patient paperwork packet they can complete in advance
  • If they have specific insurance questions, route to the billing department
  • If they ask clinical questions about hormones (risks, benefits, eligibility), let them know the provider will go over all of this at the consultation

Key Takeaway: Never tell a patient whether they are or are not a candidate for hormone therapy. That is the provider's determination after reviewing history, symptoms, and lab work. Your job is to get them scheduled.

Scenario E: "I Need My Lab Results"

Patients are often anxious about lab results. Be empathetic and clear about the process.

Recommended Script

Lab Results Script

"I completely understand wanting your results! All lab results are reviewed by [provider] first to ensure accuracy and determine if any changes are needed. Once reviewed, [provider/we] will contact you with your results, usually within [2-3 business days]. If it's been longer than that, I'll send a message to the team right now."

Guidelines

  • Never read lab values to a patient unless specifically instructed by the provider to do so, and even then, only read what the provider has approved
  • Never interpret results — "your levels look normal" or "that seems high" is outside your scope
  • If the patient is concerned about a delay, document the inquiry and escalate to the provider or clinical team lead
  • If the patient has access to a patient portal, you may remind them to check there, but clarify that the provider's interpretation is what matters

Scenario F: "I'm Having Chest Pain / Severe Headache / Difficulty Breathing / Vision Changes"

THIS IS A POTENTIAL EMERGENCY. Do not continue triaging. Do not place the patient on hold. Do not transfer the call. Deliver the emergency script immediately.

Emergency Script

Emergency Script — Memorize This

"I need you to hang up and call 911 immediately, or go to your nearest emergency room right now. Do not drive yourself. This needs emergency evaluation. Would you like me to stay on the line while you call 911?"

After the Call

  • Document the call immediately: patient name, time, symptoms reported, and that you directed them to call 911
  • Notify the provider immediately, even if the patient is not currently in the office
  • If the patient refuses to call 911, document their refusal and notify the provider immediately
  • Follow up with the patient (or their emergency contact, if authorized) within 24 hours

Emergency Red Flags — Always Direct to 911

Symptom Possible Emergency
Chest pain or pressure Myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism
Difficulty breathing or shortness of breath Pulmonary embolism, anaphylaxis, cardiac event
Sudden severe headache ("worst headache of my life") Stroke, subarachnoid hemorrhage
Sudden vision changes (blurring, loss of vision) Stroke, retinal detachment
Facial drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty Stroke (FAST criteria)
Uncontrolled bleeding Hemorrhage
Loss of consciousness or altered mental status Multiple emergent causes
Severe allergic reaction (swelling, hives, throat tightening) Anaphylaxis

4. Handling Difficult Patients

Difficult interactions are inevitable in healthcare. Patients may be frustrated by wait times, insurance denials, or their own health struggles. How you respond to these situations is a direct reflection of your professionalism and the clinic's values. Remember: their frustration is almost never about you personally.

De-Escalation Techniques

Lower Your Voice

When a patient raises their voice, the natural instinct is to match their volume. Resist this. Instead, consciously lower your voice and slow your speech. This signals calm authority and often causes the other person to lower their volume to match yours. It is one of the most effective de-escalation tools available to you.

Validate First, Solve Second

Before offering any solution or explanation, acknowledge the patient's feelings. People need to feel heard before they can hear you. Validation does not mean you agree with everything they are saying. It means you recognize their emotional experience.

  • "I can see this is really frustrating for you."
  • "I understand this is not the experience you were expecting."
  • "You have every right to be concerned about this."

Use "I" Statements

"I" statements keep the focus on what you can do, rather than placing blame or creating defensiveness. They demonstrate ownership and willingness to help.

  • Instead of: "You need to calm down" — try: "I want to make sure I help you with this"
  • Instead of: "That's not my job" — try: "I'm going to connect you with the right person who can help"
  • Instead of: "There's nothing I can do" — try: "Let me find out what options we have"

Offer Solutions, Not Excuses

Patients do not want to hear why something went wrong. They want to know what you are going to do about it. Focus your language on next steps and action.

  • "Here's what I can do for you right now..."
  • "Let me look into this and get back to you by [specific time]."
  • "I'm going to escalate this to [person/team] to make sure it gets resolved today."

Empathy Statements to Memorize

Keep these in your back pocket. Practice them until they feel natural:

  • "I can see this is frustrating, and I want to help you with this."
  • "I understand how important this is to you."
  • "Let me find out what I can do to make this right."
  • "I hear your concern, and I'm going to make sure it gets addressed."
  • "I appreciate your patience while I work on this."
  • "I would feel the same way in your situation."
  • "Thank you for letting me know about this. I want to fix it."

When to Involve a Manager

Escalate to your supervisor or office manager when:

  • A patient becomes verbally abusive or uses threatening language
  • You have attempted de-escalation and the patient remains agitated
  • The patient is requesting something outside your authority (billing disputes, policy exceptions, provider complaints)
  • You feel unsafe at any point
  • The patient demands to speak with a manager — honor this request promptly

Clinical Pearl: Never take difficult interactions personally. A patient who is angry about a billing issue or a long wait time is not angry at you as a person. They are frustrated with a situation, and you happen to be the person in front of them. Protect your own emotional well-being by recognizing this distinction.

5. HIPAA for Phone Calls

Every phone call involving patient information is subject to HIPAA regulations. Violations can result in significant fines for the clinic and disciplinary action for the individual. Understanding HIPAA compliance for phone calls is not optional — it is essential to your role.

Verifying Patient Identity

Before discussing any protected health information (PHI) over the phone, you must verify the caller's identity. The clinic standard requires at minimum two identifiers:

Primary Identifier Secondary Identifier (Pick One)
Full legal name Date of birth
Full legal name Last four digits of SSN (if on file)
Full legal name Address on file
Full legal name Phone number on file
Identity Verification Script

"Before I can access your account, I'll need to verify your identity. Can you please confirm your full name and date of birth?"

Voicemail Guidelines

Leaving voicemails requires extra caution because you cannot verify who will listen to the message.

  • Never leave detailed medical information on a voicemail — no diagnoses, lab results, medications, or treatment details
  • Keep it brief and general: clinic name, your name, and a request to call back
  • Check the patient's chart for voicemail preferences — some patients have authorized detailed messages
Safe Voicemail Script

"Hi, this is [Your Name] from [Clinic Name]. We're calling for [Patient First Name]. Could you please give us a call back at [clinic number] at your earliest convenience? Thank you."

Family Members and Third Parties

Warning: Never share patient information with a family member, spouse, parent (of adult patients), or any third party unless there is a signed HIPAA authorization form on file specifically naming that individual. "But I'm their husband/wife/mother" is not sufficient authorization.

  • If a family member calls requesting information, politely explain that you need written authorization on file
  • Offer to have the patient call you directly or to add an authorized representative through the proper form
  • Check the chart for existing HIPAA release forms before every call involving a third party

Environmental Awareness

  • Be aware of your surroundings when discussing PHI — can other patients in the waiting room hear you?
  • Use a private area or lower your voice when discussing sensitive information
  • Before discussing information, ask the patient: "Are you in a place where you can speak freely about your health information?"
  • Never discuss patient information in public areas (hallways, break rooms, elevators)

6. Medication Refill Workflow

Medication refill requests are among the most frequent calls you will handle. A clear, consistent workflow ensures that nothing falls through the cracks and patients receive their medications without unnecessary delays.

Standard Refill Process

  1. Patient calls requesting a refill — gather medication name, dose, frequency, and pharmacy name/location
  2. Verify patient identity (name + DOB) and confirm the medication is currently in their chart
  3. Document the refill request in the EHR with all details
  4. Route the message to the prescribing provider for review and approval
  5. Provider reviews and approves, denies, or modifies the request
  6. If approved: prescription is sent electronically to the pharmacy
  7. If denied or modified: follow up with the patient to explain and schedule an appointment if needed

What to Gather on the Call

Information Why It Matters
Medication name Prevents confusion between similarly-named drugs
Dose and frequency Confirms the patient is taking it as prescribed
Pharmacy name and location Ensures the prescription goes to the right place
Number of refills remaining (if patient knows) Helps provider determine if a new prescription is needed
When they last took the medication Identifies compliance issues or gaps in therapy
Any changes in symptoms or side effects Alerts provider to potential concerns

Timeline Expectations

Key Takeaway: Set clear expectations with patients. Standard refill requests are processed within 24-48 business hours. Advise patients to call at least 5-7 days before they run out of medication to allow adequate processing time.

Controlled Substances

Controlled substance refills require additional steps and cannot be handled the same way as routine medications:

  • Many controlled substances cannot be refilled by phone and require an office visit
  • Verify the patient's identity with extra care
  • Check the last fill date in the chart or prescription monitoring database
  • Controlled substances often cannot be called in to the pharmacy — they require electronic prescribing with EPCS (Electronic Prescribing for Controlled Substances)
  • Never tell a patient you will "call in" a controlled substance — route to the provider
  • If a patient reports their controlled substance was lost or stolen, document and route to the provider without making promises

7. Prior Authorization Basics

Prior authorizations (PAs) are one of the most common sources of patient frustration. When you understand the process, you can explain it clearly, set accurate expectations, and reduce anxiety for both the patient and the clinical team.

What Is a Prior Authorization?

A prior authorization is a requirement by the patient's insurance company that the provider obtain approval before the insurance will cover a specific medication, procedure, or test. It is not a denial — it is a request for the insurance company to review whether the treatment meets their criteria for coverage.

Medications That Commonly Require PA at This Clinic

Medication Category Examples PA Likelihood
GLP-1 Receptor Agonists Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy), Tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) Almost always required
Compounded Hormones Compounded testosterone, compounded progesterone, custom pellet formulations Sometimes required, varies by insurer
Brand-Name Hormones Testosterone cypionate (brand), estradiol patches (brand-specific) Occasionally, especially for brand over generic

Information Needed for a PA

  • Patient diagnosis — ICD-10 code and clinical description
  • Medication requested — name, dose, frequency, duration
  • Clinical justification — why this specific medication is medically necessary
  • Prior therapies tried and failed — many insurers require "step therapy," meaning the patient must have tried (and failed) cheaper alternatives first
  • Supporting lab work or clinical documentation — relevant to the diagnosis and treatment plan

Timeline

Prior authorizations can take anywhere from 5 to 14 business days, sometimes longer. The process involves:

  1. Clinic submits the PA request to the insurance company (often via fax or an online portal)
  2. Insurance reviews the request against their coverage criteria
  3. Insurance may request additional information (which restarts the clock)
  4. Insurance issues a decision: approved, denied, or approved with modifications
  5. Clinic notifies the patient and pharmacy of the decision

What to Tell the Patient

Prior Authorization Patient Script

"Your insurance requires prior authorization before they'll cover this medication. That means [provider] needs to submit clinical information to your insurance company for their review. This process typically takes 5 to 14 business days. We'll contact you as soon as we hear back. In the meantime, if you haven't heard from us within two weeks, please don't hesitate to call and we'll check on the status for you."

If the PA Is Denied

  • The provider will determine next steps (appeal, alternative medication, or peer-to-peer review with the insurance medical director)
  • Notify the patient of the denial and what the provider recommends as the next step
  • If appealing, let the patient know the appeal timeline (usually another 5-14 business days)
  • If switching medications, schedule a follow-up to discuss alternatives

Clinical Pearl: Patients often blame the clinic for PA delays. Help them understand that the delay is on the insurance side, not ours. We are advocating for them. Phrases like "We're working on this for you" and "We want to get this approved just as much as you do" go a long way.

9. Cultural Sensitivity

Our clinic serves a diverse patient population. Cultural sensitivity is not an add-on to good patient care — it is the foundation. Every patient deserves to feel respected, seen, and safe, regardless of their background, identity, or language.

Names and Pronouns

  • Always use the patient's preferred name — this may differ from their legal name in the chart. Check for preferred name fields in the EHR.
  • Use the patient's stated pronouns — if you are unsure, it is appropriate to ask: "What pronouns do you use?" or "How would you like to be addressed?"
  • Do not assume gender based on voice — especially on the phone. A deep voice does not mean the caller is male, and a high voice does not mean the caller is female.
  • If you make a mistake — correct yourself quickly, apologize briefly, and move on. Do not make it a bigger deal than it needs to be.

Language Barriers

  • Identify language needs early — if a patient has difficulty communicating in English, arrange for interpreter services
  • Never use family members as interpreters for clinical discussions — use a professional medical interpreter (phone or in-person) to ensure accuracy and confidentiality
  • Speak clearly and at a moderate pace — avoid slang, idioms, and complex medical jargon
  • Confirm understanding — use teach-back methods: "Can you tell me in your own words what we discussed?"
  • Know your clinic's interpreter service phone number and how to access it

Cultural Considerations in Hormone and Sexual Health

Our clinic specializes in hormone therapy, which touches on topics that carry different cultural weight for different patients:

  • Some patients may be uncomfortable discussing hormones, sexual function, or reproductive health openly — create a safe, judgment-free space
  • Some cultures have specific beliefs about hormone treatments, body modification, or gender-related care — listen without judgment
  • Privacy may be especially important — some patients may not have disclosed their treatment to family members
  • Weight and body image are sensitive topics across all cultures — be thoughtful when discussing GLP-1 medications and weight management

Inclusive Language

Instead Of Use
"What is your real name?" "What name would you like us to use?"
"Are you a man or a woman?" "How do you identify?" or use the information in the chart
"Your husband/wife" "Your partner" or "your spouse" (unless they specify)
"You don't look like you need to lose weight" Avoid commenting on appearance entirely
"That's not how we do things here" "Let me explain how our process works"

Clinical Pearl: Cultural sensitivity is a practice, not a destination. You will not always get it right, and that is okay. What matters is your willingness to learn, your respect for each patient as an individual, and your commitment to creating an environment where everyone feels welcome and safe.
