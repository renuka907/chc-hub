1. Overview & Indications

What is Colposcopy?

Colposcopy is a diagnostic procedure using a colposcope (a binocular magnifying instrument, 6-40x magnification) to examine the cervix, vagina, and vulva for signs of disease after an abnormal Pap smear or positive HPV test.

Purpose

To identify the location, size, and severity of cervical lesions and determine whether biopsy is needed. Colposcopy is NOT a screening tool — it's a diagnostic evaluation triggered by abnormal screening results.

Indications for Colposcopy

Standard Indications

  • HSIL on Pap smear
  • ASC-H (atypical squamous cells, cannot exclude HSIL)
  • LSIL on Pap (age ≥25)
  • ASC-US with positive HPV test
  • AGC (atypical glandular cells)
  • Persistent HPV 16/18 positive
  • Unsatisfactory Pap with positive HPV

Other Indications

  • Visible cervical lesion on speculum exam
  • Unexplained abnormal vaginal bleeding
  • DES exposure in utero
  • History of cervical dysplasia needing follow-up
  • Post-treatment surveillance (after LEEP/CKC)
  • Immunocompromised with abnormal screening

Contraindications

  • Active cervicitis — treat infection first, then reschedule
  • Heavy menstruation — obscures visualization
  • Pregnancy — colposcopy can be performed but NO ECC (endocervical curettage)

2. Cervical Anatomy Review

Key Anatomical Landmarks

Diagram: Normal cervical anatomy with clock orientation (patient supine)

Ectocervix

Covered by squamous epithelium (pink, smooth, shiny). This is the visible portion of the cervix during speculum exam.

Endocervix

Lined by columnar epithelium (red, grape-like/villous). Located within the cervical canal above the os.

The Transformation Zone (T-Zone)

The area between the original and new squamocolumnar junction (SCJ) where columnar epithelium undergoes squamous metaplasia. This is where ~90% of cervical neoplasia originates. The entire T-zone MUST be visualized for a satisfactory colposcopy.

Squamocolumnar Junction (SCJ) Types

SCJ TypeVisibilityClinical Significance
Type 1Fully visible on ectocervixEntire T-zone visible — satisfactory colposcopy
Type 2Partially in endocervical canalMay need endocervical speculum to visualize
Type 3Entirely within canal, not visibleUnsatisfactory colposcopy — consider ECC or LEEP

3. The Colposcopy Procedure

Colposcopy Setup
Figure: Colposcopy examination showing speculum placement and colposcope positioning. Source: Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Step-by-Step Procedure

StepActionPurpose
1Insert speculum, visualize cervixBaseline assessment of cervix
2Apply normal salineRemove mucus, assess surface vessels with green filter
3Apply 3-5% acetic acid (vinegar)Causes protein coagulation → abnormal areas turn WHITE
4Wait 60-90 seconds, observeAcetowhite changes become visible; note intensity, borders, location
5Apply Lugol's iodine (Schiller test)Normal epithelium stains BROWN; abnormal stays YELLOW
6Colposcopic assessment & scoringGrade findings, identify worst area
7Biopsy if indicatedTarget most abnormal area(s); consider ECC
8Apply hemostatic agent (Monsel's/silver nitrate)Control biopsy site bleeding

Key Principle: Acetic Acid Response

Why it works: Acetic acid causes reversible coagulation of nuclear proteins. Areas with higher nuclear density (dysplasia, neoplasia) have more protein → turn whiter and more opaque.

  • Thin, translucent acetowhite → likely low-grade (CIN 1 / LSIL)
  • Dense, opaque, oyster-white → likely high-grade (CIN 2-3 / HSIL)
  • Rapid onset (appears quickly, fades slowly) → more severe

C. Nabothian Cysts

Nabothian Cysts — Gross Appearance

Smooth, round, yellow-white translucent cysts on the cervix. Formed when squamous metaplasia covers columnar glands, trapping mucus. Completely benign — no treatment needed.

Nabothian Cysts — Histology

H&E stain showing mucus-filled cystic spaces lined by endocervical-type epithelium, surrounded by mature squamous epithelium. Benign retention cysts.

Normal Findings Summary

  • Smooth, pink squamous epithelium — no acetowhite changes
  • Visible SCJ (entire T-zone seen)
  • Columnar epithelium / ectropion — red, grape-like; NOT abnormal
  • Nabothian cysts — benign retention cysts; round, translucent
  • Mild, thin, translucent acetowhite in immature metaplasia — normal
  • Fine, regular vascular patterns

5. Abnormal Findings — Visual Guide

A. Acetowhite Epithelium — CIN 1 (Low-Grade)

CIN 1 — Acetowhite Change After Acetic Acid (VIA Positive)
Figure: Cervix showing acetowhite epithelium after acetic acid application (VIA positive). White patches visible on the cervical surface indicate areas of increased nuclear density consistent with CIN 1. Note the thin, translucent quality and irregular borders — characteristic of low-grade disease. Source: Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Thin / Translucent Acetowhite (LOW GRADE)

  • Faint white, semi-transparent
  • Irregular, feathered borders
  • Appears slowly after acetic acid
  • Fades quickly
  • Suggests: CIN 1 / LSIL

Dense / Opaque Acetowhite (HIGH GRADE)

  • Thick, oyster-white, opaque
  • Sharp, well-defined borders
  • Appears rapidly after acetic acid
  • Persists >1 minute
  • Suggests: CIN 2-3 / HSIL

B. Vascular Patterns

Fine Punctation (LOW GRADE)

Evenly spaced, small, uniform red dots. Represents capillary loop tips viewed end-on. Regular intercapillary distance.

Coarse Punctation (HIGH GRADE)

Irregularly spaced, dilated, varying-sized dots. Wide, uneven intercapillary distance. Vessels appear dilated and prominent.

Fine Mosaic (LOW GRADE)

Regular, small, evenly-sized "tiles" or "chicken wire" pattern. Vessels form a uniform grid.

Coarse Mosaic (HIGH GRADE)

Irregular, large, uneven tile pattern. Thick, prominent vessel borders with variable block sizes.

C. Cervical Polyps

Cervical Polyp — Clinical Appearance

Smooth, red/pink, pedunculated growth protruding from the cervical os. Most are benign (endocervical polyps). Can cause irregular bleeding, post-coital spotting, or vaginal discharge.

Endocervical Polyp — Histology

H&E stain showing polypoid tissue with endocervical-type glands, fibrovascular stroma, and chronic inflammatory cells. Benign — but always send for histology to rule out malignancy.

Images: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0 / CC BY 4.0)

Cervical Polyps — Key Points

  • Prevalence: ~2-5% of women; more common in multiparous women aged 40-60
  • Types: Endocervical (most common) or ectocervical
  • Presentation: Often asymptomatic; may cause intermenstrual bleeding, post-coital bleeding, or mucous discharge
  • Management: Simple polypectomy (twist and remove with ring forceps); send ALL polyps for histology
  • Malignancy risk: <1% — but adenocarcinoma can present as a polyp, so ALWAYS histologically evaluate
  • Recurrence: ~6-12% may recur; follow-up Pap smear recommended

D. Atypical Vessels

Red Flag Finding — Atypical Vessels

Bizarre, irregular, non-branching vessels that do NOT follow normal patterns:

  • Comma-shaped or corkscrew vessels
  • Spaghetti-like — long, hairpin turns
  • Abrupt caliber changes
  • Irregular branching — does not dichotomize normally

→ Highly suspicious for invasive cancer. MUST BIOPSY immediately.

E. Ridge Sign & Inner Border Sign

Ridge Sign (Raised Edge)

An opaque, raised ridge at the SCJ within a lesion. Represents the demarcation between CIN 2 and CIN 3. Highly predictive of CIN 2+.

Inner Border Sign

A well-demarcated area of denser acetowhite within a larger acetowhite lesion — a "lesion within a lesion." The inner border is higher-grade disease. Biopsy the inner lesion.

F. Cervical Cancer — Gross Pathology

Red Flags Suspicious for Invasive Cancer

  • Atypical vessels — bizarre, non-branching, corkscrew pattern
  • Ulceration or necrosis — irregular surface erosion
  • Exophytic or papillary mass
  • Dense acetowhite + coarse vessels + irregular surface
  • Bleeds easily on contact (friable tissue)

→ Biopsy IMMEDIATELY. Refer to gynecologic oncology if cancer confirmed.

Cervical Carcinoma — Gross Pathology

Gross specimen showing advanced cervical carcinoma with visible tumor mass. The normal cervical anatomy is distorted by the neoplastic growth.

Cervical Carcinoma with Adnexa

Important Distinction

Cytology (Pap smear) = screening → Bethesda terminology (LSIL, HSIL)
Histology (biopsy) = diagnostic → CIN grading (CIN 1, 2, 3)
Don't mix them up in documentation!

CIN Grading — Histology Images

CIN 1 — Mild Dysplasia

Histology showing dysplastic cells confined to the lower 1/3 of the epithelium. Upper layers show normal maturation. Most CIN 1 regresses spontaneously (~60-70% within 2 years).

CIN 3 — Severe Dysplasia / CIS

Histology showing dysplastic cells occupying full thickness of the epithelium. Loss of normal maturation pattern. High nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio throughout. Requires treatment.

7. Swede Scoring System

Standardized colposcopic scoring system to predict histological grade. Score ≥5 is highly predictive of CIN 2+.

ParameterScore 0Score 1Score 2

Lesion Size <5mm or <1 quadrant 5-15mm or 1-2 quadrants >15mm, 3-4 quadrants, or undefined

8. Biopsy Decision-Making

When to Biopsy

ASCCP 2019 Guidelines

  • Biopsy the worst area — target the densest acetowhite, coarsest vessels
  • ≥2 biopsies improve sensitivity — at least 2 targeted biopsies when lesion is large or multifocal
  • Random biopsies of normal-appearing T-zone acceptable if referral cytology was HSIL/ASC-H
  • ECC — perform if SCJ not fully visible or colposcopy unsatisfactory

Biopsy Technique

  • Use punch biopsy forceps (Tischler or Baby Tischler)
  • Biopsy at the edge of the worst-looking area
  • Get adequate depth — include stroma beneath epithelium
  • Label specimens separately (e.g., "12 o'clock biopsy," "6 o'clock biopsy")
  • Apply Monsel's solution or silver nitrate for hemostasis
  • Never biopsy during pregnancy without OB consultation

When NOT to Biopsy

  • Normal colposcopy with low-risk referral (ASC-US/HPV+) — can defer
  • Active cervicitis — treat first
  • Pregnancy — ECC absolutely contraindicated; ectocervical biopsy only if cancer suspected

Treatment Options

Excisional (Diagnostic + Therapeutic)

  • LEEP / LLETZ — most common; loop excision
  • Cold knife conization (CKC) — preferred for AIS
  • Laser conization

Advantage: Provides tissue for histology

Ablative (Destroys tissue)

  • Cryotherapy — freezing; small CIN 1-2
  • Thermal ablation
  • Laser ablation

Limitation: No specimen; only if entire lesion visible, no invasion suspected

  2. Informed consent obtained & documented
  3. LMP confirmed — rule out pregnancy if applicable
  4. Speculum placed — cervix visualized
  5. Saline applied → assess native vessels (green filter)

  11. ECC if indicated
  12. Hemostasis achieved
  13. Document ALL findings with clock positions
  14. Patient instructions given (verbal + written)
  15. Schedule follow-up

Clinical Photo Atlas — Polyps & Cysts

Cervical Polyps

Cervical polyp — pedunculated, red-pink growth protruding from the cervical os. Smooth surface, well-vascularized. Most are benign endocervical polyps arising from endocervical glands. Can cause intermenstrual bleeding, post-coital spotting, or mucous discharge.

Resected fibroglandular cervical polyp, anterior view. Smooth, well-circumscribed, pedunculated mass. This is a pathology specimen from polypectomy. Always send for histological evaluation.

Same polyp, posterior view. Note the stalk (pedicle) where it attached to the cervical canal. Fibroglandular polyps contain both glandular and fibrous tissue elements.

Microscopic view showing endocervical-type glands within a fibrovascular stroma. Scattered chronic inflammatory cells. Benign. Malignancy rate in cervical polyps is <1%.

Cervical Polyp — Ultrasound (Power Doppler)

Transvaginal ultrasound with power Doppler showing a cervical polyp with its feeding blood vessel highlighted in orange/red. Useful for pre-procedure assessment.

Nabothian Cysts

Nabothian Cysts — Clinical Appearance

Round, smooth, yellow-white translucent cysts on the cervix surface. Formed when squamous metaplasia covers and traps mucus-secreting columnar glands. Completely benign — no treatment needed. Very common finding.

Nabothian Cysts — Histology (H&E)

Mucus-filled cystic spaces lined by a single layer of endocervical-type columnar epithelium. Surrounded by mature squamous epithelium. Benign retention cysts — no clinical significance.

Clinical Photo Atlas — Abnormal Findings

VIA (Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid) — Normal vs Abnormal

VIA Negative (Normal)

Cervix after acetic acid application shows no acetowhite changes. The epithelium remains uniformly pink. This is a normal result — no dysplasia suspected.

VIA Positive (CIN 1)

Cervix after acetic acid showing acetowhite patches — white areas indicate increased nuclear density. This case shows CIN 1 (low-grade) changes. The acetowhite areas have somewhat irregular borders and thin/translucent quality.

What to Look For After Acetic Acid

  • Timing: Wait a full 60-90 seconds after applying 3-5% acetic acid
  • Location: Is the acetowhite change within the transformation zone? Near the SCJ?
  • Intensity: Faint/translucent (low-grade) vs dense/oyster-white (high-grade)
  • Borders: Irregular/feathered (low-grade) vs sharp/well-defined (high-grade)
  • Speed: Slow onset & quick fade (low) vs rapid onset & persistent (high)
  • Don't confuse: Immature metaplasia can show faint acetowhite — this is NORMAL

CIN Histology — Low-Grade vs High-Grade

CIN 1 — Mild Dysplasia (Low-Grade)

Dysplastic cells confined to lower 1/3 of epithelium. Upper 2/3 shows normal maturation with decreasing nuclear size. Koilocytes (HPV cytopathic effect) often present. 60-70% regress spontaneously within 2 years.

CIN 3 — Severe Dysplasia / CIS (High-Grade)

Dysplastic cells occupy full thickness. No normal maturation pattern. High nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, frequent mitoses, nuclear pleomorphism throughout. Basement membrane still intact (not invasive). Treatment required.

Clinical Photo Atlas — Cervicitis

Cervicitis (inflammation of the cervix) must be identified and treated before performing colposcopy, as it can obscure findings and cause false-positive acetowhite changes.

Infectious Cervicitis

Cervix showing mucopurulent discharge, erythema, and friability consistent with infectious cervicitis. Common causes: Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Trichomonas vaginalis, HSV. Treat infection first, then reschedule colposcopy.

Herpetic Cervicitis (HSV)

Cervix showing herpetic ulcerations — shallow, irregular erosions with surrounding erythema. HSV cervicitis can mimic dysplasia on colposcopy. Look for vesicles, ulcers, and severe pain as distinguishing features.

Cervicitis — Documentation Points

  • Appearance: Erythema, edema, friability, mucopurulent discharge
  • Testing: NAAT for chlamydia/gonorrhea, wet prep for trich, HSV culture if ulcers
  • Management: Treat per CDC STI guidelines → reschedule colposcopy in 4-6 weeks
  • Colposcopy contraindicated: Active cervicitis → treat first, then evaluate

When to Suspect Invasive Cancer on Colposcopy

  • Visible mass — exophytic, papillary, or ulcerated
  • Atypical vessels — corkscrew, comma, spaghetti patterns
  • Contact bleeding — bleeds easily when touched (friable)
  • Necrosis — areas of tissue death, foul odor
  • Dense acetowhite + coarse vessels + raised surface

Action: Biopsy immediately → Refer to gynecologic oncology

Clinical Photo Atlas — Treatment

Cervical Cryotherapy

Cryotherapy — a cryoprobe is applied to the cervix to freeze and destroy abnormal tissue. Used for small, fully visible CIN 1-2 lesions when the entire lesion and SCJ are visible. The freeze-thaw-freeze technique is standard. Limitations: no tissue specimen for histology; not for glandular disease, suspected invasion, or large lesions.

Cysts remain visible as raised, smooth structures. The overlying epithelium may show faint acetowhite due to metaplasia — this is normal, not dysplasia.

Cervical Polyp — Colposcopic Views (IARC Atlas)

Mucous polyps are the most common type — a localized overgrowth of endocervical columnar epithelium. On colposcopy, they appear as single or multiple soft reddish masses protruding from the external os.

Cervical Polyp — Colposcopic View

Soft, reddish mass protruding from the os. The polyp is usually mobile and can be pushed in different directions to visualize the SCJ behind it.

Inflamed Cervical Polyp

Polyps can be inflamed and congested, bleeding easily on contact. Can be removed by twisting with sponge-holding forceps. Always send for histology.

Polyp — Acetowhite (Metaplasia)

Polyps can be acetowhite because of metaplastic changes on the surface. This is usually benign — but must be distinguished from dysplasia on the polyp.

Polyp — Acetowhite Close-Up

Close-up of acetowhite changes on a cervical polyp surface after acetic acid. The acetowhite pattern here is from metaplasia.

Polyp with Dense Acetowhite Area

Rarely, a premalignant lesion may appear as a dense acetowhite area on the polyp. Also note: an acetowhite area on the cervix may be concealed behind a polyp — always look behind it!

Polyp — After Lugol's Iodine

A polyp does NOT stain with Lugol's iodine (remains yellow/unstained) because it's covered by columnar epithelium, which lacks glycogen. Surrounding squamous epithelium stains brown.

Fibroid polyp: Sometimes cervical or even corporal fibroids may extrude from the os. Fibroid polyps have a smooth surface, pale whitish color, and firm consistency. Unlike mucous polyps, they are NOT as mobile and usually have a thick stem. Source: IARC/WHO Atlas of Colposcopy

Condyloma (HPV Warts) — Colposcopic View

Condyloma — After Saline

Raised, papillomatous (finger-like) projections on the cervix. Caused by low-risk HPV types (usually 6, 11). May appear as white, pink, or flesh-colored raised lesions.

Condyloma — After Acetic Acid

Turns bright white with characteristic papillomatous surface — "cauliflower-like" or finger-like projections. Distinguished from flat acetowhite lesions by the raised, irregular surface contour.

Leukoplakia — Colposcopic View

Leukoplakia — Before Acetic Acid

White patch visible on the cervix BEFORE applying acetic acid. This is the key distinction — leukoplakia is visible with saline alone (keratinized epithelium). Can be benign OR can overlie dysplasia.

Leukoplakia — After Acetic Acid

White patch persists and may become more prominent. Always biopsy leukoplakia — it may be benign hyperkeratosis, but can hide CIN 2-3 or even cancer underneath the keratin layer.

Leukoplakia — Key Teaching Point

Leukoplakia = white BEFORE acetic acid. Acetowhite = white AFTER acetic acid. Don't confuse them!

  • Leukoplakia is visible as a white plaque on the native cervix (with saline)
  • It represents keratinization — abnormal for the cervix (which is normally non-keratinized)
  • May be benign (hyperkeratosis, chronic irritation) OR may overlie dysplasia
  • ALWAYS biopsy — you can't see through it with acetic acid

LSIL / CIN 1 — Colposcopic View

After Saline

Cervix appears mostly normal. Subtle changes may be difficult to see without magnification.

After Acetic Acid

Thin, translucent acetowhite patches with irregular borders. Slow onset. Fades relatively quickly.

After Lugol's Iodine

Patchy iodine uptake — areas of faint yellow or partial staining mixed with brown. Helps delineate borders.

HSIL / CIN 2-3 — Colposcopic View

After Saline

May show subtle surface irregularity and abnormal vascular patterns even before acetic acid. Green filter helps visualize vessels.

After Acetic Acid

Dense, opaque acetowhite with sharp, well-defined borders. Rapid onset. Note the difference in intensity vs the LSIL images above.

After Acetic Acid (Close-up)

Dense acetowhite lesion with sharp margins. May show coarse punctation or mosaic pattern within. This is CIN 2 — biopsy required.

Source: IARC/WHO Atlas of Colposcopy — Case AABH (38yo, HPV+, histology: HSIL-CIN2)

Invasive Cervical Cancer — Colposcopic View

Invasive Cancer — After Saline

Irregular, friable cervix with abnormal surface contour. May show necrosis, ulceration, or exophytic growth. Bleeds easily on contact.

Invasive Cancer — After Acetic Acid

Dense acetowhite with atypical vessels — irregular, dilated, non-branching. Surface may show erosion, papillary projections, or necrotic areas. Biopsy immediately → Gyn-Onc referral.

Close-up view of invasive cervical cancer. Note the irregular surface, atypical vessel patterns, and tissue destruction. This is what we're trying to PREVENT through screening and early colposcopy. Source: IARC/WHO Atlas
