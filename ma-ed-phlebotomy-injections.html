1. Order of Draw & Tube Guide

The order of draw is a standardized sequence established by CLSI (Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute) to prevent cross-contamination of additives between tubes. Incorrect order of draw can cause clinically significant errors in lab results, especially for coagulation studies and electrolyte panels. In an HRT and wellness clinic, accuracy is critical because treatment decisions (dosing testosterone, adjusting thyroid medication, titrating GLP-1 agonists) are made directly from these lab values.

Why Order Matters in HRT Clinics

The most commonly drawn tubes in an HRT/wellness clinic are Gold (SST) for hormone panels and Lavender (EDTA) for CBC/HbA1c. If you draw a lavender tube before a gold tube, EDTA can contaminate the gold tube and falsely lower calcium and magnesium levels. If a light blue tube is needed (less common in wellness, but occasionally ordered for patients on anticoagulants), it must be drawn second and filled completely to maintain the correct blood-to-citrate ratio.

Clinical Pearl When drawing only a light blue top tube (no blood culture first), you must draw a discard tube first. Use a plain red top or a second light blue top as the discard. This clears tissue thromboplastin from the needle puncture that would otherwise contaminate the citrate tube and skew coagulation results.

Tube Fill Requirements

Underfilled tubes are a common reason for specimen rejection. Each tube has a minimum fill volume:

  • Light blue (citrate): Must be filled to at least 90% of the draw volume. A short draw here is an automatic reject because the blood-to-anticoagulant ratio is critically important for accurate coagulation testing.
  • Gold (SST): Fill to the indicated line. Minimum is typically 3.5 mL for a standard 5 mL tube. Under-filled SSTs may not produce enough serum after centrifugation.
  • Lavender (EDTA): Fill to the minimum line (usually 1 mL for a 3 mL tube). Overfilling causes clumping; underfilling causes cell shrinkage from excess EDTA.
  • Gray (fluoride): Must be adequately filled so that fluoride concentration properly inhibits glycolysis. Underfilling causes falsely low glucose results.
Warning Never pour blood from one tube into another. Never remove stoppers to add blood. If a tube is underfilled, re-draw the specimen rather than risk inaccurate results that could lead to incorrect dosing adjustments.

Specimen Handling After Collection

  • Gold SST tubes: Allow to clot for 30 minutes at room temperature before centrifuging. Do not centrifuge early or clots will form in the serum, causing instrument errors.
  • Lavender EDTA tubes: Mix well immediately. Can be stored at room temperature for up to 24 hours for CBC, but HbA1c specimens are more stable if refrigerated.
  • Gray tubes: Mix immediately. Fluoride inhibits glycolysis within the tube, but mixing is critical for even distribution of the additive.
  • Light blue tubes: Must be tested within 4 hours of collection, or 24 hours if refrigerated (check your reference lab's requirements).

3. Difficult Draws & Troubleshooting

Not every venipuncture is straightforward. Developing strategies for difficult draws is one of the most valuable skills an MA can have. Confidence in handling challenging veins reduces patient anxiety, minimizes repeat sticks, and keeps the clinic running on time.

Identifying Difficult Veins

Common Challenging Populations in HRT/Wellness Clinics

  • Patients on GLP-1 agonists with significant weight loss: May have loose skin over veins; veins can roll more easily without subcutaneous fat anchoring them.
  • Patients with higher BMI: Veins are often deeper and harder to palpate visually; rely on palpation rather than visualization.
  • Dehydrated patients (fasting labs): Fasting patients are commonly dehydrated, which makes veins smaller and less prominent. Encourage water intake before fasting labs (water does not break a fast).
  • Older patients: Fragile, thin-walled veins that are prone to bruising and blowing. Skin is often less elastic.
  • Patients with a history of IV drug use: Scarred veins, difficult access. Approach without judgment.
  • Patients with anxiety: Vasovagal responses, tense muscles making palpation harder, moving target.

Techniques for Difficult Veins

Warming the Site

Apply a warm compress or disposable hand warmer wrapped in a paper towel to the antecubital fossa for 3–5 minutes. Heat causes vasodilation and can increase vein diameter by 20–40%. This is the single most effective technique for difficult draws.

Anchoring Rolling Veins

For veins that roll laterally when you attempt to puncture:

  1. Apply the tourniquet and identify the vein by palpation.
  2. Place your non-dominant thumb 1–2 inches below the intended puncture site.
  3. Pull the skin taut downward (toward the wrist), stretching the skin and anchoring the vein.
  4. Approach at a 15–30 degree angle and enter with a swift, confident motion.
  5. Do not release your anchoring thumb until the needle is in the vein and you have flash.

Butterfly Needle Technique for Small or Fragile Veins

Use a 23-gauge butterfly (winged infusion set) for:

  • Hand veins when antecubital veins are not accessible
  • Elderly patients with fragile veins
  • Patients who have failed a straight needle attempt
  • Pediatric or anxious patients when applicable

When using a butterfly: insert at a very shallow angle (10–15 degrees), advance slowly until you see flash in the tubing, then gently lower the wings flat against the skin and tape in place before attaching tubes. The dead space in butterfly tubing means the first 0.5 mL of blood is used to fill the tubing; account for this when determining if you have enough specimen volume.

Clinical Pearl If using a butterfly to fill a light blue top (citrate) tube, you must use a discard tube first to account for the dead space in the butterfly tubing. If the dead space volume dilutes the citrate tube, coagulation results will be inaccurate. A small red top or a second light blue top works as the discard.

Vein Palpation Techniques

  • Use your index finger, not your thumb. Your thumb has its own pulse and can confuse you into thinking you are feeling a vein when you are actually feeling your own heartbeat over an artery.
  • Palpate with the pad of your finger, not the tip. The pad has more nerve endings and better tactile sensitivity.
  • A vein feels spongy and bouncy when pressed. An artery pulses. A tendon feels hard and cord-like and does not bounce. If the structure you feel is hard and does not give when pressed, it is a tendon — do not stick it.
  • Have the patient pump their fist 5–10 times to encourage venous filling, then have them make a fist and hold it (not pumping continuously, which can alter potassium levels).

When You Cannot Find a Vein

  1. Check the other arm before moving to hand veins.
  2. Check the lateral forearm (cephalic vein often runs along the outer forearm).
  3. Ask the patient: "Which arm do you usually have drawn from?" or "Has anyone ever had difficulty drawing your blood?" Patients know their own veins better than you do.
  4. Lower the arm below heart level and let gravity assist with filling.
  5. If two attempts fail, ask a colleague to try. Never attempt more than twice per phlebotomist — this is both a professional standard and a kindness to the patient.
Warning Never draw from an arm with: a functioning AV fistula or graft (dialysis access), lymphedema or history of mastectomy with lymph node removal on that side, an IV line running (specimen will be diluted), or a hematoma from a prior attempt (draw proximal to the hematoma is inaccurate and painful).

Preventing Hemolysis

Hemolyzed specimens are the most common cause of specimen rejection in clinical labs. Hemolysis falsely elevates potassium, LDH, AST, and iron while falsely decreasing some analytes. To prevent hemolysis:

  • Use the appropriate gauge needle for the vein size (21-gauge is standard; 23-gauge for small veins).
  • Do not use excessive tourniquet time (remove within 1 minute if possible).
  • Allow alcohol to dry completely before puncture (wet alcohol entering the puncture site hemolyzes cells).
  • Do not pull back on the syringe plunger too forcefully if using a syringe method.
  • Gently invert tubes — do not shake them vigorously.
  • Avoid drawing through a hematoma.
  • Avoid using veins where the patient has been applying a warm compress for more than 10 minutes (prolonged heat can cause hemolysis).

Managing Vasovagal Reactions

Some patients faint or feel faint during blood draws. This is a vasovagal syncope response and is more common in anxious patients, fasting patients, and patients with a history of fainting during draws.

Prevention

  • Always ask: "Have you ever had any problems with blood draws? Have you ever fainted?"
  • If they say yes, have them lie down flat for the draw. Never draw a syncopal-risk patient in a chair without arms.
  • Encourage conversation to distract them (ask about their day, pets, weekend plans).
  • Have the patient look away from the needle and the tubes filling.

If a Patient Starts to Feel Faint

  1. Remove the tourniquet and withdraw the needle immediately.
  2. Apply pressure to the site.
  3. Recline the patient or lower their head between their knees if seated.
  4. Apply a cool, damp cloth to the back of their neck or forehead.
  5. Offer juice or water once they feel better.
  6. Do not leave the patient unattended until they are fully recovered.
  7. Document the incident in the chart.

4. Subcutaneous Injections

Subcutaneous (SQ or SubQ) injections deposit medication into the adipose tissue layer between the skin and muscle. Absorption from subcutaneous tissue is slower and more predictable than intramuscular injection, which is ideal for medications like GLP-1 receptor agonists, peptides, and HCG that require steady, sustained absorption.

Injection Sites for Subcutaneous Administration

Abdomen (Preferred for most SQ injections)

  • Location: At least 2 inches away from the umbilicus in any direction. The best area is the fatty tissue in a semicircle around the navel, avoiding the midline.
  • Advantages: Consistent absorption, easy self-injection access, large area for rotation.
  • Site rotation: Divide the abdomen into quadrants and rotate sites systematically. Do not inject into the same spot within a 1-inch radius for at least 1 week.

Thigh (Anterior/Lateral)

  • Location: Outer middle third of the thigh, between the knee and the hip. Find the area by dividing the thigh into thirds from the knee to the hip; use only the middle third.
  • Advantages: Good alternative for self-injection; large surface area.
  • Note: Absorption may be slightly slower from the thigh than the abdomen. Patients who exercise heavily should be aware that vigorous leg exercise after injection can accelerate absorption.

Upper Arm (Posterior)

  • Location: The fatty area on the back of the upper arm, between the shoulder and elbow.
  • Disadvantages: Difficult for self-injection (hard to reach and pinch with one hand). Best used when a caregiver or MA is administering.

SQ Injection Technique Step-by-Step

  1. Verify the order. Confirm the medication, dose, route, patient identity (two identifiers), and expiration date.
  2. Gather supplies: Alcohol swab, medication vial or pen, appropriate syringe and needle (insulin syringe or 1 mL syringe with 27–31 gauge, 1/2-inch needle), bandage, sharps container.
  3. Hand hygiene. Wash hands or use alcohol-based hand sanitizer. Glove up.
  4. Prepare the medication. If drawing from a vial, clean the rubber stopper with alcohol and let dry. Draw up the prescribed dose, ensuring no air bubbles. For pre-filled pens, prime per manufacturer instructions.
  5. Select and clean the site. Clean with an alcohol swab in a circular motion from the center outward. Allow to air dry completely (30 seconds).
  6. Pinch the skin. Use your non-dominant hand to gently pinch a 1–2 inch fold of skin and subcutaneous tissue. This lifts the adipose layer away from the underlying muscle.
  7. Insert the needle. Hold the syringe like a dart at a 45-degree or 90-degree angle depending on body composition:
    • 45-degree angle: For thin patients or when using a longer needle (5/8 inch) to ensure you stay in subcutaneous tissue and do not penetrate into muscle.
    • 90-degree angle: For patients with adequate adipose tissue using a 1/2-inch needle. This is the most common approach.
  8. Inject the medication. Push the plunger slowly and steadily. A slow injection is less painful and allows even distribution in the tissue.
  9. Wait before withdrawing. For most medications, hold the needle in place for 5–10 seconds after completing the injection. For GLP-1 pens, the manufacturer may recommend holding for 10 seconds to ensure the full dose is delivered.
  10. Withdraw and dispose. Withdraw the needle at the same angle it was inserted. Do not recap. Immediately place in the sharps container. Apply gentle pressure with gauze if needed (do not rub — rubbing can cause bruising and alter absorption).
  11. Document. Record the medication, dose, route, site, lot number, expiration date, and any patient response in the EHR.

5. Intramuscular Injections

Intramuscular (IM) injections deliver medication directly into muscle tissue, where rich blood supply allows for relatively rapid and predictable absorption. In HRT/wellness clinics, the two most common IM medications are testosterone cypionate and vitamin B12 (cyanocobalamin or methylcobalamin).

Testosterone Cypionate IM Injection

Key Medication Facts

  • Concentration: Typically 200 mg/mL (most common) or 100 mg/mL.
  • Vehicle: Cottonseed oil or grape seed oil. The oil vehicle makes the medication viscous (thick), requiring a larger gauge needle for drawing up.
  • Typical dose range: 50–200 mg per injection, administered every 1–2 weeks (or divided into twice-weekly doses for more stable levels).
  • Storage: Room temperature (do not refrigerate — cold oil is even more viscous and harder to inject).

Key Takeaway Always use separate needles for drawing up and injecting. The drawing needle (18G) dulls against the rubber stopper and would cause unnecessary pain if used for injection. Attach the smaller injection needle after drawing up the medication. This technique also prevents tracking of the medication through subcutaneous tissue.

Injection Sites for IM Administration

Ventrogluteal (Preferred for Most IM Injections)

The ventrogluteal site is the gold standard for IM injections in adults. It has the thickest, most developed muscle mass, minimal subcutaneous fat relative to other sites, and no major nerves or blood vessels in the area.

How to locate the ventrogluteal site:

  1. Have the patient lie on their side with the upper leg slightly bent, or stand with weight shifted to the opposite leg.
  2. Place the heel of your hand on the greater trochanter (the bony prominence on the side of the hip).
  3. Point your index finger toward the anterior superior iliac spine (the front hip bone).
  4. Spread your middle finger posteriorly toward the iliac crest, forming a V shape.
  5. The injection site is the center of the triangle formed by your index finger, middle finger, and the iliac crest.

Deltoid

The deltoid is suitable for injections of 1 mL or less. It is commonly used for B12 injections and can be used for testosterone cypionate when the dose volume is small (0.5 mL or less).

How to locate the deltoid site:

  1. Have the patient relax their arm at their side or rest it on their hip.
  2. Find the acromion process (the bony prominence at the top of the shoulder).
  3. Measure 2–3 finger widths below the acromion process.
  4. The injection site is the thickest part of the deltoid muscle, forming an inverted triangle.
  5. Do not inject too low (risk of hitting the radial nerve) or too high (risk of shoulder injury related to injection, or SIRVA).
Warning — SIRVA (Shoulder Injury Related to Vaccine Administration) SIRVA occurs when an injection is administered too high in the deltoid, injecting medication into the subdeltoid bursa or rotator cuff structures. This causes severe, prolonged shoulder pain and limited range of motion. Always inject 2–3 finger widths BELOW the acromion process — never at or above shoulder level.

Dorsogluteal (Use with Extreme Caution)

The dorsogluteal site (upper outer quadrant of the buttock) was traditionally used for IM injections, but is now discouraged by most clinical guidelines because of the risk of sciatic nerve injury and the higher amount of subcutaneous fat in this area (which means the medication may not actually reach the muscle). If your provider asks you to use this site, ensure proper landmarking: draw an imaginary line from the posterior superior iliac spine to the greater trochanter; inject above and lateral to this line.

Vastus Lateralis (Lateral Thigh)

The outer middle third of the thigh. Used for self-injection of testosterone by patients who prefer to inject into their thigh. The muscle is large and accessible, but patients may experience more soreness compared to the gluteal region.

Z-Track Technique

The Z-track technique is recommended for all IM injections, but is especially important for oil-based medications like testosterone cypionate to prevent the medication from leaking back through the injection tract into the subcutaneous tissue (which causes pain, irritation, and subtherapeutic dosing).

Z-Track Procedure

  1. Prepare the injection as normal. Attach the clean injection needle.
  2. Select and clean the injection site. Allow to dry.
  3. Using the side of your non-dominant hand, pull the skin and tissue laterally (to the side), approximately 1–1.5 inches away from the injection site. Hold firmly.
  4. While holding the skin displaced, insert the needle at a 90-degree angle with a smooth, dart-like motion.
  5. Inject the medication slowly and steadily (approximately 10 seconds per mL for oil-based medications).
  6. Wait 10 seconds after completing the injection before withdrawing the needle. This allows the medication to begin absorbing into the muscle tissue.
  7. Withdraw the needle smoothly at the same angle.
  8. Release the displaced skin. The tissue layers slide back into alignment, creating a zigzag path that seals the injection tract and prevents medication from leaking out.
  9. Apply gentle pressure with gauze. Do not massage the site (massage pushes the medication back through the Z-track).
Clinical Pearl For testosterone cypionate: inject very slowly. Oil-based medications are viscous and injecting too fast causes significant post-injection pain (PIP). A good rule of thumb is 10 seconds per 0.5 mL. After injecting, count to 10 before withdrawing the needle. This patience pays off — patients who receive slow, careful injections have far less soreness and are more compliant with their injection schedule.

Vitamin B12 Injection

Key Medication Facts

  • Forms: Cyanocobalamin (most common, 1000 mcg/mL) or Methylcobalamin (active form, 1000–5000 mcg/mL, light-sensitive).
  • Route: IM or deep SQ (provider preference).
  • Typical dose: 1000 mcg (1 mL of 1000 mcg/mL solution) weekly or monthly.
  • Storage: Room temperature for cyanocobalamin. Methylcobalamin must be protected from light and may require refrigeration — check the compounding pharmacy label.

B12 Injection Technique

  • Needle: 25G x 1 inch for deltoid; 22–25G x 1–1.5 inch for ventrogluteal.
  • Site: Deltoid is the most common choice for B12 because the volume is small (1 mL) and the medication is a thin, water-based solution that injects easily.
  • Technique: Standard IM technique. B12 is not oil-based, so it injects smoothly without the resistance of testosterone.
  • Z-track: Recommended but less critical than for oil-based injections since B12 is thin and absorbs quickly.
Key Takeaway Deltoid vs. Ventrogluteal decision: Use the deltoid for volumes up to 1 mL of thin solutions (B12, vaccines). Use the ventrogluteal for volumes up to 3 mL and for viscous/oil-based medications (testosterone cypionate). The deltoid muscle simply cannot accommodate large volumes or thick oils without significant pain.

6. Peptide Reconstitution with Bacteriostatic Water

Many peptides used in HRT/wellness clinics arrive as lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder in a sealed vial. Before they can be injected, they must be reconstituted (mixed) with bacteriostatic water (BAC water). This is a precise process that requires understanding the math behind concentration calculations to ensure patients receive the correct dose.

What is Bacteriostatic Water?

Bacteriostatic water (BAC water) is sterile water that contains 0.9% benzyl alcohol as a preservative. The benzyl alcohol inhibits bacterial growth, which means the reconstituted peptide can be stored for multiple uses (typically 28–30 days when refrigerated). Do NOT use regular sterile water for injection (SWI) unless specifically instructed — SWI contains no preservative and must be used within 24 hours of reconstitution or discarded.

Reconstitution Equipment

  • Peptide vial (lyophilized powder)
  • Bacteriostatic water vial (typically 30 mL multi-use vial)
  • Alcohol swabs
  • 1 mL or 3 mL syringe with an 18G or 21G needle (for drawing up BAC water)
  • Insulin syringe (for measuring doses after reconstitution)
  • Sharps container

Step-by-Step Reconstitution

  1. Verify the peptide. Check the label: peptide name, total amount in the vial (in milligrams or micrograms), and expiration date. Check the provider's order for the prescribed dose.
  2. Determine the volume of BAC water to add. This is usually specified by the compounding pharmacy or the provider. Common reconstitution volumes are 1 mL, 2 mL, 3 mL, or 5 mL, depending on the peptide and desired concentration. (See math section below.)
  3. Clean the tops of both vials (peptide and BAC water) with separate alcohol swabs. Allow to dry.
  4. Draw up the BAC water. Using the syringe, draw up the prescribed volume of BAC water. Pull back the plunger to the correct mark, then insert the needle into the BAC water vial (inverted) and slowly draw the water to the mark.
  5. Add BAC water to the peptide vial. Insert the needle into the peptide vial at a slight angle so the water runs down the inside wall of the vial. Do NOT aim the stream directly at the powder cake — this can damage the peptide structure.
  6. Inject the water slowly. Let the BAC water flow gently down the glass wall. Do not squirt it forcefully.
  7. Swirl gently to dissolve. Once the water is added, gently roll or swirl the vial between your palms. NEVER shake the vial — shaking can denature (break down) the peptide and reduce its effectiveness. The powder should dissolve within 1–3 minutes. If undissolved particles remain, let the vial sit in the refrigerator for 15–30 minutes and then gently swirl again.
  8. Label the vial. Write on the vial or affix a label with: the date of reconstitution, the concentration (e.g., "250 mcg per 0.1 mL"), the patient name if applicable, and the discard date (28 days from reconstitution unless otherwise specified).
  9. Store properly. Reconstituted peptides must be refrigerated (36–46 degrees F / 2–8 degrees C). Do not freeze. Keep away from light.
Warning Never shake a peptide vial. Peptides are delicate protein chains that can be denatured (broken) by vigorous shaking. A denatured peptide is ineffective. Always gently swirl or roll the vial. If the solution appears cloudy or has visible particles after swirling for several minutes, do not use it — contact the pharmacy.

Reconstitution Math

Understanding reconstitution math is essential. The goal is to determine the concentration of the reconstituted solution so you can calculate how many units or milliliters to draw up for the prescribed dose.

The Core Formula

Concentration = Total peptide in vial / Volume of BAC water added

Example 1: BPC-157

Vial contains: 5 mg (5000 mcg) of BPC-157
BAC water to add: 2 mL
Prescribed dose: 250 mcg twice daily

Step 1: Calculate concentration.
5000 mcg / 2 mL = 2500 mcg per mL

Step 2: Convert to insulin syringe units. An insulin syringe reads in "units" where 100 units = 1 mL. So:
2500 mcg / 100 units = 25 mcg per unit

Step 3: Calculate the dose volume.
250 mcg / 25 mcg per unit = 10 units on the insulin syringe

The patient draws to the "10" mark on a 100-unit insulin syringe for each 250 mcg dose.

Example 2: CJC-1295/Ipamorelin Blend

Vial contains: 6 mg (6000 mcg) total blend (3 mg CJC + 3 mg Ipamorelin)
BAC water to add: 3 mL
Prescribed dose: 300 mcg of the blend daily at bedtime

Step 1: Concentration = 6000 mcg / 3 mL = 2000 mcg per mL

Step 2: Per insulin unit = 2000 mcg / 100 units = 20 mcg per unit

Step 3: Dose = 300 mcg / 20 mcg per unit = 15 units

Example 3: HCG

Vial contains: 10,000 IU of HCG
BAC water to add: 5 mL
Prescribed dose: 500 IU three times per week

Step 1: Concentration = 10,000 IU / 5 mL = 2000 IU per mL

Step 2: Per insulin unit = 2000 IU / 100 units = 20 IU per unit

Step 3: Dose = 500 IU / 20 IU per unit = 25 units

Example 4: Semaglutide (Compounded)

Vial contains: 5 mg semaglutide lyophilized
BAC water to add: 2.5 mL
Prescribed dose: 0.25 mg (250 mcg) weekly

Step 1: Concentration = 5 mg / 2.5 mL = 2 mg per mL = 2000 mcg per mL

Step 2: Per insulin unit = 2000 mcg / 100 units = 20 mcg per unit

Step 3: Dose = 250 mcg / 20 mcg per unit = 12.5 units (draw to halfway between 12 and 13 on the syringe)

Key Takeaway — Quick Reconstitution Math Shortcut If you add 1 mL of BAC water to any vial, the concentration per insulin unit is simply: total peptide / 100. If you add 2 mL, divide by 200. If you add 5 mL, divide by 500. The denominator is always the volume in mL multiplied by 100 (the number of units in 1 mL on an insulin syringe).

How Many Doses Per Vial?

This is a question patients frequently ask. Using Example 1 (BPC-157, 5 mg vial, 250 mcg dose):

5000 mcg total / 250 mcg per dose = 20 doses per vial
At twice daily dosing, that is 10 days per vial.

Using Example 3 (HCG, 10,000 IU vial, 500 IU dose):

10,000 IU / 500 IU per dose = 20 doses per vial
At 3 times per week, that is approximately 6.7 weeks per vial. However, the vial must be discarded 28 days after reconstitution, so the patient will get approximately 12 doses (4 weeks x 3 injections per week) before discarding.

Clinical Pearl Always remind patients that reconstituted peptides must be discarded 28 days after mixing, regardless of how much is left in the vial. The benzyl alcohol in BAC water only suppresses bacterial growth for approximately 28 days. After that, contamination risk increases. Some pharmacies specify shorter windows (14 days for certain peptides) — always defer to the pharmacy label.

7. Sharps Safety & Disposal

Sharps safety is a critical component of clinical practice and is regulated by OSHA's Bloodborne Pathogens Standard (29 CFR 1910.1030). Every MA must understand and follow sharps safety protocols to protect themselves, their patients, and their coworkers from needlestick injuries and potential bloodborne pathogen exposure.

OSHA Requirements

  • Sharps containers must be puncture-resistant, leak-proof, and labeled with the biohazard symbol. They must be easily accessible, upright, and not overfilled past the fill line (typically 2/3 to 3/4 full).
  • Needles must never be recapped. The single most common cause of needlestick injuries is recapping needles. If a safety mechanism exists on the needle (retractable or hinged), activate it immediately after use.
  • Sharps containers must be at the point of use — within arm's reach of where injections and blood draws are performed. Do not carry used needles across the room to a sharps container.
  • Sharps containers should be replaced when they reach the fill line. Overfilled containers are a needlestick hazard.

What Goes in a Sharps Container

  • Used needles (all types and sizes)
  • Used lancets
  • Used butterfly needles and attached tubing
  • Broken glass that may be contaminated
  • Used scalpel blades (including trocar blades from pellet procedures)
  • Any rigid item that could puncture skin and is contaminated or potentially contaminated

Needlestick Injury Protocol

If you experience a needlestick injury:

  1. Immediately wash the wound with soap and running water for at least 5 minutes. If the exposure is to a mucous membrane (eyes, nose, mouth), flush with water or saline for at least 15 minutes.
  2. Do not squeeze the wound to "push out" blood — this can cause more tissue damage and does not reduce infection risk.
  3. Report the incident immediately to your supervisor.
  4. Complete an incident report per your clinic's protocol.
  5. Seek medical evaluation within 1–2 hours. Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) for HIV is most effective when started within 2 hours and must be started within 72 hours.
  6. The source patient's blood should be tested (with consent) for HIV, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C.
  7. Your blood should be drawn for baseline testing for HIV, Hepatitis B surface antibody, and Hepatitis C.
  8. Follow-up testing at 6 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months post-exposure.
Warning Do not delay medical evaluation after a needlestick. HIV PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis) involves a 28-day course of antiretroviral medication and is most effective when started within 2 hours. Every hour of delay reduces effectiveness. This is urgent — treat it that way.

Patient Sharps Disposal Education

Patients who self-inject at home need to be educated on proper sharps disposal. Many municipalities have specific laws about how sharps must be handled in the home:

  • FDA-cleared sharps container: Available at pharmacies for $5–10. This is the safest option.
  • Alternative container: A heavy-duty plastic container with a screw-top lid (such as a laundry detergent bottle). Label it "SHARPS — DO NOT RECYCLE."
  • Disposal options: Drop-off at designated collection sites (pharmacies, hospitals, fire stations), mail-back programs, or household hazardous waste collection events.
  • Never throw loose needles in the trash or recycling. This is illegal in most states and dangerous to sanitation workers.

8. Teaching Patients Self-Injection

Many HRT and wellness patients will need to self-inject at home. Your role as an MA includes teaching them proper technique, building their confidence, and ensuring they feel comfortable before they leave the clinic. This is one of the most impactful patient education opportunities you will have.

Principles of Effective Self-Injection Teaching

Assess Readiness

  • Ask about needle phobia or anxiety. Normalize it: "Many patients are nervous at first. That's completely normal."
  • Assess dexterity: arthritis, tremors, visual impairment may require a caregiver to assist.
  • Determine learning style: some patients learn best by watching, others by doing.

The Teach-Back Method

The most effective teaching technique for clinical education:

  1. Demonstrate: Perform the injection yourself on a practice pad or the patient's first injection, narrating each step.
  2. Have them practice: Give the patient a syringe (capped) and have them practice drawing up saline or show them on a practice pad.
  3. Watch them do it: On their second injection (or a practice session), observe them perform each step independently, only intervening if there is a safety concern.
  4. Ask them to teach you: Have the patient walk YOU through the process as if teaching a friend. This is the teach-back — if they can accurately describe each step, they truly understand it.

Self-Injection Teaching Checklist

For Subcutaneous Self-Injection (GLP-1, Peptides, HCG)

  1. Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water.
  2. Gather supplies: medication vial (or pen), alcohol swab, insulin syringe, sharps container.
  3. Check medication label: right medication, right dose, not expired, solution is clear (not cloudy).
  4. Clean the vial top with an alcohol swab (if using a vial).
  5. Draw up the correct dose (review how to read the syringe).
  6. Remove air bubbles (tap syringe gently, push air out).
  7. Select injection site (abdomen, thigh) and clean with alcohol. Wait for it to dry.
  8. Pinch the skin.
  9. Insert the needle at the correct angle (90 degrees for most patients).
  10. Inject slowly.
  11. Wait 5–10 seconds.
  12. Remove the needle and apply gentle pressure (do not rub).
  13. Dispose of the needle in a sharps container immediately. Never recap.
  14. Record the date, time, dose, and site in an injection log (provide a paper or digital log template).

For IM Self-Injection (Testosterone Cypionate)

  1. Wash hands, gather supplies: testosterone vial, drawing needle (18G), injection needle (22–25G depending on site), alcohol swabs, bandage, sharps container.
  2. Clean the vial top.
  3. Draw air equal to the dose into the syringe, inject air into the vial (prevents vacuum).
  4. Invert the vial, draw up the prescribed dose. The oil is thick — draw slowly.
  5. Remove bubbles by tapping. Ensure the dose is accurate.
  6. Swap the drawing needle for the injection needle (twist off, twist on). Do not contaminate the sterile injection needle.
  7. Select and clean the site (ventrogluteal or vastus lateralis for self-injection).
  8. Using Z-track technique, pull skin laterally.
  9. Insert at 90 degrees with a swift, confident motion.
  10. Inject very slowly (10 seconds per 0.5 mL).
  11. Wait 10 seconds.
  12. Withdraw, release skin, apply gentle pressure.
  13. Dispose of both needles in sharps container.
Clinical Pearl Give patients a written instruction sheet with pictures showing the injection site, angle, and steps. Even the most attentive patient will forget verbal instructions when they are home alone with the needle for the first time. Written and visual instructions are retained much better than verbal alone. Many compounding pharmacies provide injection instruction cards — include these in the patient's take-home packet.

Common Patient Concerns and Responses

Patient ConcernResponse
"What if I hit a nerve?""The injection sites we've selected are chosen specifically to avoid nerves. If you feel a sharp, shooting, or electric pain, stop immediately, withdraw the needle, and try a slightly different spot."
"What if I see blood?""A small amount of bleeding at the injection site is normal. Apply gentle pressure with a gauze pad. If you see blood flash back into the syringe, it means you may be in a blood vessel — withdraw and try again at a new site."
"Can I reuse needles?""No. Use a new needle for every injection. Reusing needles causes pain (the tip dulls after one use), increases infection risk, and can cause tissue damage."
"The injection site is red/sore.""Mild redness and soreness for 1–2 days is normal, especially with IM injections. Ice the area for 10–15 minutes. Contact us if you see increasing redness, warmth, swelling, or drainage, which could indicate infection."
"I'm scared of the needle.""That's very common. Try taking a few deep breaths before injecting. Some patients find it helps to count to three and inject on three. With practice, it becomes routine — most patients tell us it gets much easier after the first few times."
"What if I inject an air bubble?""A small air bubble in a subcutaneous or intramuscular injection is not dangerous. The air is absorbed by the tissue. However, try to remove air bubbles before injecting for dose accuracy."

Injection Site Rotation

Teach patients the importance of rotating injection sites to prevent:

  • Lipohypertrophy: Hard, rubbery lumps under the skin from repeated injection in the same spot. Medication does not absorb well from lipohypertrophic tissue, leading to unpredictable drug levels.
  • Lipoatrophy: Loss of subcutaneous fat at the injection site, creating a visible dip or depression in the skin.
  • Scar tissue: Repeated trauma to the same area causes fibrosis that makes future injections more painful and absorption less reliable.

Provide patients with a rotation diagram or chart where they can check off which site they used on which date.
